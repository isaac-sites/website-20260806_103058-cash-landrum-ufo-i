## Introduction

Before Betty Cash, [Vickie Landrum]({{ 'vickie-landrum/' | relative_url }}) and [Colby Landrum]({{ 'colby-landrum/' | relative_url }}) took their case to federal court, they spent more than two years trying to obtain relief through political and military administrative channels. Their route was not simply “report a UFO, then sue”. They wrote to Texas senators, were directed to the Judge Advocate office at Bergstrom Air Force Base, gave a recorded statement to Air Force lawyers, filed separate compensation claims and appealed after those claims were rejected.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-overview"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/cash_landrum_ufo_inc_db9d45_administrative_claim_4bc9ad-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Claims Process" width="1024" height="559" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Illustrative overview</span></figcaption></figure>

That sequence matters because federal law generally requires a claimant to present a monetary claim to the responsible agency before suing the United States. The Cash–Landrum administrative record therefore became the procedural bridge between the alleged encounter of December 1980 and the later civil action. It also exposed the central difficulty that would follow the witnesses into court: the Air Force could process a claim alleging government responsibility without accepting that any government aircraft, personnel or programme had caused the injuries.

## Elected representatives opened the official route

During 1981, Cash sought assistance from Texas senators Lloyd Bentsen and John Tower. The surviving correspondence shows that the senators did not independently investigate the reported object or promise compensation. Instead, their offices consulted defence authorities and directed Cash towards the Air Force claims system.

Bentsen wrote on 28 July 1981 that, after conversations with Department of Defense representatives, Cash should contact the Judge Advocate Claims Officer at Bergstrom Air Force Base in Austin “to file an official report and to submit a claim”. He added that officials at Bergstrom had been made aware of her letter and general situation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlanC.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cashlan C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cashlan C</span></span></span>

Tower provided similar guidance in a letter dated 4 September 1981, supplying the address of Bergstrom’s Base Staff Judge Advocate and Claims Officer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlanC.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cashlan C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cashlan C</span></span></span> The correspondence is significant not because it confirmed the witnesses’ account, but because it moved their complaint from informal political correspondence into an established compensation channel.

This was a common form of congressional assistance. An elected representative could ask an agency where jurisdiction lay, ensure that a constituent reached the correct office and seek a response. The representative did not adjudicate the facts or compel payment. In the Cash–Landrum case, congressional intervention therefore produced access rather than a finding of responsibility.

## What happened at Bergstrom

Cash, Vickie Landrum and seven-year-old Colby met Air Force legal personnel at Bergstrom on 17 August 1981. The recorded interview took place in the base law library with Captain John Camp, the acting Staff Judge Advocate; Captain Terry Davis, the claims officer; and assistant claims officer Pat Wolf.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2...</span></span></span>

The meeting served two purposes that are sometimes confused. First, the officials took a detailed account of the reported encounter, including its location, [the object]({{ 'the-object/' | relative_url }}), the heat and the helicopters. Secondly, they explained how the witnesses could submit claims for compensation. It was not a renewed Project Blue Book investigation and did not amount to an Air Force acceptance of their allegations.

Camp told the witnesses that the Air Force no longer maintained an office for investigating UFO sightings and that he had no authority at base level to create such an inquiry. He nevertheless offered to forward a report and to provide the documents needed to start a claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 2 of 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 2 of 2...</span></span></span> That distinction shaped everything that followed: the legal office could receive and process a damages demand even though the service denied having a programme responsible for determining what the object had been.

The officials also made clear that they were government lawyers, not the witnesses’ personal advisers. Davis recommended consulting a civilian solicitor or attorney before completing the forms. Camp explained that each claimant would need a separate form, supporting material and a written account, and assured them that their applications would receive the same administrative consideration as other claims against the government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 2 of 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 2 of 2...</span></span></span>

## From interview to formal compensation claims

The witnesses did not submit completed claims immediately after the August 1981 interview. The preserved forms were signed on 20 December 1982, almost two years after the alleged exposure and close to the statutory deadline stated on the form itself. Peter Gersten, their civilian lawyer, transmitted the three claims and supporting medical documents to Bergstrom later that month. His covering letter is dated 23 December; an Air Force historical summary records the claims as formally presented on 27 December 1982.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlanC.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cashlan C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cashlan C</span></span></span>

The timing reflected an important feature of federal claims practice. A person seeking damages from the United States normally has to present an administrative demand to the appropriate agency before commencing an action under the Federal Tort Claims Act. The submission must identify the incident and demand a specific monetary sum; the agency must deny the claim, or fail to decide it within the statutory period, before a lawsuit may proceed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title28-section2675" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CodeJuly 17, 2026 — 28 USC 2675: Disposition by federal agency as prerequisite; evidence Text contains those laws in effect on July 17, 2...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 17, 2026</span></span></span>

Each Cash–Landrum form was addressed to the Base Staff Judge Advocate at Bergstrom. Each described the claimant’s position in [the car]({{ 'the-car/' | relative_url }}), the hovering object, intense heat and the subsequent appearance of about 23 military-type helicopters, several apparently of the tandem-rotor type. The forms converted the witnesses’ narrative into allegations designed to establish government liability: an unidentified aerial object caused injury, military-looking helicopters were associated with it, and the United States should compensate the injured parties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlanC.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cashlan C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cashlan C</span></span></span>

That wording did not solve the [attribution problem]({{ 'attribution-problem/' | relative_url }}). The forms could describe “military-type” helicopters, but they could not name a unit, base, crew, federal employee or recognised aircraft programme. The administrative process therefore began with a complete account of what the witnesses believed had happened but without direct documentary proof that a federal agency controlled the object or helicopters.

The claims were forwarded beyond Bergstrom to the Air Force’s central Claims and Tort Litigation Staff. In March 1983, the chief of its Torts Branch acknowledged receipt and stated that a staff attorney had been assigned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlanC.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cashlan C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cashlan C</span></span></span> This transfer shows that the submissions were treated as formal legal claims rather than merely filed as UFO reports.

## Denial, appeal and the limits of the record

The Air Force rejected the claims. The publicly available file is frustratingly incomplete at the most important point: portions explaining the internal reasons for denial were withheld or redacted when the documents were released under the Freedom of Information Act. A later Air Force summary records that the claims were appealed on 22 July 1983, but the entries stating the reasons for the original decisions and the rejected appeals are obscured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlanC.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cashlan C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cashlan C</span></span></span>

The surviving appeal decisions are exceptionally brief. Separate documents for Cash, Vickie Landrum and Colby state that their appeals concerning injuries allegedly resulting from an unidentified flying object and unidentified helicopters were considered under 10 U.S.C. §2733 and denied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlanC.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cashlan C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cashlan C</span></span></span> They contain no public discussion of the medical evidence, helicopter identification, witness credibility or possible alternative causes.

There is also a minor documentary inconsistency. The historical summary lists 21 August 1983 as the date the appeals were denied, whereas the three surviving action sheets appear to have been signed on 31 August.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlanC.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cashlan C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cashlan C</span></span></span> The discrepancy does not alter the outcome, but it illustrates why precise chronologies of the case should distinguish between dates recorded in later summaries and dates visible on individual documents.

The governing authority cited in the denial sheets, 10 U.S.C. §2733, concerns administrative settlement of certain claims arising from non-combat activities of the armed forces. That choice is notable because the public record often describes the later lawsuit principally through the Federal Tort Claims Act. At the administrative stage, however, Air Force lawyers appear to have processed the submissions through a military claims framework before the dispute moved into ordinary federal tort litigation.

## How denial cleared the way for court

The administrative proceedings did not establish that the witnesses had encountered a government vehicle. Nor did they produce compensation, a public evidential hearing or a detailed explanation of why the Air Force considered the claims deficient. Their practical effect was procedural: the witnesses had presented monetary demands to the military, received adverse decisions and exhausted an internal appeal.

Once that route closed, Gersten could frame the dispute as a civil claim against the United States rather than an unanswered request for agency assistance. Later Air Force correspondence identifies the resulting federal matter as *Cash et al. v. United States*, Civil Action No. H-84-348, confirming that litigation was under way by 1984.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlanC.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cashlan C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cashlan C</span></span></span>

The progression can therefore be understood in three stages:

1. **Political referral:** Bentsen and Tower directed the witnesses to the military office capable of receiving claims.
2. **Administrative presentment:** [Bergstrom recorded]({{ 'bergstrom-interview/' | relative_url }}) their account, supplied claims forms and forwarded the completed applications for central Air Force review.
3. **Exhaustion and escalation:** rejection of the claims and appeals removed the prospect of an administrative settlement and led to a federal civil action.

The claims process also foreshadowed the decisive weakness of the lawsuit. The witnesses could document that they had reported injuries and formally demanded compensation. What they could not independently demonstrate was the necessary connection between those injuries and an identifiable act, employee or property of the United States. Administrative review did not resolve that evidential gap; it merely carried it forward into federal court.
