## Introduction

The compensation claims arising from the Cash–Landrum incident were not filed immediately after the alleged encounter of 29 December 1980. Instead, Betty Cash, [Vickie Landrum]({{ 'vickie-landrum/' | relative_url }}) and Colby Landrum submitted formal [administrative claims]({{ 'claims-process/' | relative_url }}) to the U.S. Air Force in December 1982, almost two years later. That delay has often been portrayed as suspicious or inexplicable, but the surviving administrative record points to a more procedural explanation. The witnesses spent much of 1981 attempting to identify the correct government office, gathering medical documentation and learning how claims against the federal government had to be presented. Only after civilian attorney Peter Gersten assembled the supporting material were the claims submitted shortly before the apparent two-year filing deadline imposed by the Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blueblurrylines.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blueblurrylines.com">[blueblurrylines.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blueblurrylines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blue Blurry LinesBlue Blurry LinesDecember 28, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 28, 2025</span></span></span>

## The Gap Between the Encounter and Filing

The nearly two-year interval was not a period of inactivity. Before filing formal compensation claims, the witnesses pursued several less formal avenues.

Following the reported encounter, [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }}) sought assistance from members of Congress rather than immediately beginning legal proceedings. Correspondence from Texas senators directed her to the Judge Advocate's office at Bergstrom Air Force Base, where Air Force legal officers interviewed the witnesses in August 1981 and explained the administrative claims process. That interview produced information about how to pursue compensation, but it did not itself constitute a formal damages claim.

The Air Force officers also advised the witnesses that they were government lawyers rather than private counsel and suggested consulting a civilian attorney before completing the necessary paperwork. That recommendation became important because a valid FTCA claim required considerably more than simply reporting an unusual incident. It had to identify the claimant, describe the alleged government responsibility and request a specific monetary amount.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cufon-o.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[cufon.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Other Files&quot; Directory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CUFON &quot;Other Files&quot; Directory...</span></span></span>

## Why December 1982 Became the Critical Moment

The timing of the filing closely matched the legal framework governing federal tort claims.

Under the FTCA, an administrative claim generally must be presented to the responsible federal agency within two years after the claim accrues. Failure to meet that deadline normally bars any later lawsuit. Courts have consistently treated this requirement as a fundamental prerequisite to litigation against the United States.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/721/503/162420/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[justia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mary L. Wardsworth, Plaintiff-appellant, v. United States of America, Defendant-appellee, 721 F.2d 503 (5th Cir. 1983):: Justia...</span></span></span>

Because the Cash–Landrum incident occurred on 29 December 1980, a filing in December 1982 placed the claims just inside that apparent statutory window. The surviving record therefore suggests that the witnesses were working against a legal deadline rather than choosing an arbitrary filing date.

The timing also explains why the administrative claims became such an important procedural milestone. Without first presenting those claims to the Air Force, the later federal lawsuit could not proceed under the FTCA.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/721/503/162420/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[Justia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mary L. ## Peter Gersten's Role in Completing the Submission

The formal claims were submitted only after attorney Peter Gersten became involved.

Gersten, already known for representing clients in UFO-related matters, helped organise the material into formal compensation demands. Contemporary accounts and later case summaries consistently describe him as the lawyer who prepared and filed the administrative claims on behalf of the three witnesses in December 1982. Those claims sought substantial monetary compensation for alleged physical injuries and related losses, supported by [medical records]({{ 'medical-record/' | relative_url }}) and witness documentation assembled over the preceding months.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blueblurrylines.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blueblurrylines.com">[Blue Blurry Lines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blueblurrylines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blue Blurry LinesBlue Blurry LinesDecember 28, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 28, 2025</span></span></span>

His involvement also addressed a practical problem identified during the Bergstrom meeting. Air Force officials had explained the procedural requirements but could not act as the claimants' legal advisers. A private attorney therefore became responsible for transforming witness statements and medical evidence into legally sufficient administrative claims.

## Delay Does Not Necessarily Indicate Doubt

The late filing has sometimes been interpreted as evidence that the witnesses lacked confidence in their account. The administrative record supports a more limited conclusion.

Several practical factors help explain the elapsed time:

* **Identifying the correct process.** The witnesses initially pursued congressional assistance before learning that the Air Force claims system was the appropriate administrative route.
* **Collecting supporting records.** Medical documentation and other evidence had to be assembled before presenting substantial personal injury claims.
* **Obtaining legal representation.** The Air Force itself recommended consulting private counsel, and the claims were ultimately completed after Peter Gersten became involved.
* **Meeting procedural requirements.** FTCA claims required a properly completed submission, including a specific monetary demand rather than a general complaint. Courts have repeatedly held that incomplete filings can fail to satisfy the statute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/721/503/162420/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.justia.com">[justia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mary L. Wardsworth, Plaintiff-appellant, v. United States of America, Defendant-appellee, 721 F.2d 503 (5th Cir. 1983):: Justia...</span></span></span>

None of these factors establishes whether the underlying allegations were true or false. Instead, they explain why the administrative paperwork appeared relatively late despite the witnesses having contacted government officials much earlier.

## What the Filing Delay Meant for the Administrative Case

The December 1982 submissions marked the transition from an informal search for answers to a legally defined compensation claim.

By filing just before the apparent two-year deadline, the claimants preserved the procedural possibility of bringing an FTCA lawsuit if the Air Force rejected their demands. The subsequent litigation ultimately failed because the courts concluded there was insufficient evidence that the United States was responsible for the alleged aircraft or injuries, not because the administrative claims had been filed too late.
