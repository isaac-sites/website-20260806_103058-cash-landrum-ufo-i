## What the witnesses felt and touched

Cash described the heat as immediate, intense and difficult to escape. In the August 1981 interview at Bergstrom Air Force Base, she said the light and heat were so strong that she believed she would have been safer outside than inside the car. She placed the vehicle roughly 130 feet from [the object]({{ 'the-object/' | relative_url }}) and estimated that the encounter lasted about 15 to 17 minutes, although both figures were retrospective estimates rather than measurements taken at the scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

After the object began to rise, she said she restarted the car and used the air conditioning because the occupants were “burning up”. That detail matters because it places the alleged heating not only on exposed skin or an exterior fitting, but within the passenger compartment itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

Her statement does not independently establish the metal’s temperature, but it reinforces the witnesses’ central claim that the car body had absorbed substantial heat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

These reported effects are mechanically plausible as a *pattern* if a strong radiant source was positioned in front of and above the car. Radiation could heat exposed paint, metal trim, glass and the forward interior without first raising the entire volume of cabin air evenly. The windscreen would not necessarily block all incoming thermal energy: visible and infrared radiation can heat the glass, dashboard and occupants directly, after which those surfaces warm the surrounding air. What cannot be calculated from the testimony is the required heat flux, because the object’s true distance, duration, emission spectrum, orientation and movement are all uncertain.

## The coat, door handle and dashboard claims

The most concrete exterior claim concerned the driver’s door. Cash said that when she returned to the car, the handle was too hot for her bare hand. She therefore used the pocket of her leather jacket as insulation while opening the door. This is a specific, memorable action rather than a general recollection that the night felt hot, and it appears in the recorded Air Force interview.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/CashLandrum1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash Landrum1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">if you agree with the description of that object would you please sign and date it too?... A painfully hot metal handle would not by itself require melting temperatures. Human skin can find metal intolerably hot well before the component suffers visible damage, particularly because metal conducts heat rapidly into the point of contact. The coat claim is therefore compatible with a considerably lower temperature than would be required to distort steel, blister paint or damage seals. Its evidential weakness is different: there was no thermometer reading, recorded burn to Cash’s palm or immediate inspection of the handle.

The dashboard assertion was potentially more important. Cash told the interviewers that Landrum’s fingerprints remained embedded where she had pressed her hands against the dashboard when the car stopped. Asked whether the dashboard had melted, Cash answered cautiously: “I guess that the dashboard melted”, then repeated that the fingerprints were still present. The wording is significant. It documents her interpretation of the marks, but it does not show that she knew the material’s prior condition or had established that heat caused the impressions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

## Did the reported effects form a consistent heat pattern?

Taken together, the claims have an internally understandable sequence. A powerful source ahead of the car could first heat the occupants and forward-facing surfaces. Exterior trim and the roof or door frame might become painful to touch. Energy passing through or absorbed by the windscreen could heat the upper dashboard. If the padded material became sufficiently pliable, pressure from a hand could leave a persistent depression.

That sequence is not the same as proof. The accounts do not supply enough information to determine whether the claimed dashboard effect is compatible with the reported exposure time and distance. A useful calculation would require the object’s effective temperature or radiant output, its size, the proportion of energy absorbed by the car, windscreen transmission, surface colours, ambient temperature, air movement and the thermal properties of each component. Most of these inputs were never recorded, while the location and distance estimates themselves remained uncertain.

There is also a tension between the severity of the asserted heating and the limited reported damage. If the dashboard truly softened deeply within minutes because of an external source, investigators might reasonably look for a broader gradient of thermal effects: deformation of adjacent padding, damaged instrument trim, warped seals, discoloured upholstery, altered paint, degraded adhesives or heat effects on objects lying in the cabin. The later vehicle account instead identifies the hand-shaped dashboard marks as the only visible anomaly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufology-news.com/u/18672430/Ufology_News/The_Cash-Landrum_Incident_-_J.Schuessler.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufology-news.com">[Ufology News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufology-news.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ufology News THE CASH-LANDRUMTHE CASH-LANDRUM UFO INCIDENT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The only visible anomaly was some very clear hand-shaped imprints in the padded dashboard on the passenger (right)... That does not make the heat account impossible. Different components respond very differently to the same exposure, and a painful handle need not show permanent damage. ## Why the vehicle evidence could not be verified

The greatest problem was delay. By the August 1981 Air Force interview, nearly eight months had passed, and the car was not present. Cash said it was in Alabama because she had flown to Texas and was no longer able to make the long drive. The officials could therefore hear the claim but could not inspect the dashboard during the interview.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

Civilian investigators did later examine the vehicle. A published account associated with investigator [John Schuessler]({{ 'john-schuessler/' | relative_url }}) states that clear hand-shaped impressions were visible on the passenger side of the padded dashboard. It also says a Geiger counter was passed over the car without detecting radiation above background level, and that a handheld compass revealed no unusual strong magnetic field.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufology-news.com/u/18672430/Ufology_News/The_Cash-Landrum_Incident_-_J.Schuessler.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufology-news.com">[Ufology News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufology-news.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ufology News THE CASH-LANDRUMTHE CASH-LANDRUM UFO INCIDENT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The only visible anomaly was some very clear hand-shaped imprints in the padded dashboard on the passenger (right)... Those checks were limited. A Geiger counter reading months after the incident could address only certain forms of residual radioactive contamination. It could not confirm or exclude ordinary radiant heat, ultraviolet exposure, transient electromagnetic effects or ionising radiation that did not leave the car contaminated. Likewise, a compass is a crude screen for a persistent magnetic anomaly, not a comprehensive material or electrical examination.

More importantly, the car lacked a secure forensic chain of custody. It remained in normal private possession, was moved across state lines and was not sealed against alteration or routine wear. There is no published pre-incident photograph showing that the dashboard was unmarked, no immediate post-incident image with a verified date, and no laboratory report identifying heat-induced changes in the material. Later claims that investigators photographed the impression conflict with other summaries stating that no reliable photograph survives in the accessible record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kb42.info/index.php/Cash-Landrum_Incident_--_Physical_Evidence%3A_Dashboard_Handprint_and_Vehicle_Analysis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kb42.info">[kb42.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kb42.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kb42.info.</span></span></span>

The investigation also missed the opportunity to examine the coat and door handle as related evidence. The jacket might have shown scorching, discolouration or transferred material had it contacted an exceptionally hot fitting, although ordinary use and subsequent cleaning would quickly weaken such evidence. The handle and surrounding paint could have been checked for local heat damage and compared with unaffected parts of the car. No documented controlled analysis of either item is available.

## Could the reported heat have left physical proof?

Yes. Had the car been preserved immediately, the heat claims offered several routes to physical verification. Investigators could have mapped surface damage, sampled polymers, analysed paint and adhesives, inspected electrical systems and experimentally reproduced the dashboard impression using a matching vehicle component. Even negative findings would have been valuable, because they could have placed upper limits on how hot particular surfaces became.

Instead, the most dramatic alleged trace was never converted into quantified evidence. The dashboard marks showed, at most, that hand-shaped depressions were reportedly present when investigators later saw the car. They did not establish when the marks formed, how much force produced them or whether heat was involved. The hot handle and coat remain uninstrumented recollections, while the absence of broader vehicle damage complicates any claim that the cabin underwent extreme heating.

The fairest assessment is therefore narrower than either complete acceptance or dismissal. Cash and Landrum gave detailed, mutually related accounts of intense heat affecting their bodies, the metal car body and the interior. Their description contains a plausible thermal sequence and includes specific physical actions that were recorded in a formal interview. But because the vehicle was neither promptly secured nor scientifically tested, the case lost what might have been its clearest independent evidence. The alleged heat effects remain an important part of the Cash–Landrum narrative, but not verified physical proof of the source that the witnesses believed they encountered.
