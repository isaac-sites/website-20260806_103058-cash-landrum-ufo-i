## Introduction

One of the most debated medical questions arising from Betty Cash's reported illness after the Cash–Landrum incident is whether her later hair loss should be regarded as evidence of radiation injury. The surviving [medical record]({{ 'medical-record/' | relative_url }}) does not resolve that question. However, it does contain an important alternative explanation that is frequently overlooked: a reported scalp biopsy interpreted as consistent with **[alopecia areata]({{ 'alopecia-areata/' | relative_url }})**, a recognised autoimmune disorder that causes patchy, non-scarring hair loss. This finding does not prove that radiation played no role in Cash's illness, but it does provide an established medical diagnosis capable of explaining the pattern of alopecia without requiring ionising radiation. As a result, the biopsy occupies a central place in later debates over the medical interpretation of her case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10581488/files/Reliability_I-12_Posner.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">I. ## When marked hair loss appeared

The chronology of Cash's [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}) is important because it differs from many later retellings of the incident. According to the medical narrative reproduced by investigator [John Schuessler]({{ 'john-schuessler/' | relative_url }}) and later analysed by critics such as Gary Posner, Dr V. B. Shenoy specifically noted that there was "little, if any" hair loss when Cash was first admitted to Parkway Hospital on 2 January 1981. Pronounced alopecia became a documented issue only during her later January admission rather than on her initial presentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10581488/files/Reliability_I-12_Posner.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">I. Case Studies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">I. Case StudiesJune 16, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 16, 2025</span></span></span>

That timing matters because delayed hair loss is compatible with more than one medical mechanism. Radiation-induced epilation can occur days or weeks after sufficiently large local [radiation doses]({{ 'radiation-dose/' | relative_url }}), but autoimmune alopecia areata can likewise develop over a relatively short period and may follow physiological or emotional stress. The delayed appearance of hair loss therefore cannot, by itself, distinguish between the competing explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001450.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medlineplus.gov">[MedlinePlus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medlineplus.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Medline Plus Alopecia areata: Medline Plus Medical Encyclopedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alopecia areata: MedlinePlus Medical EncyclopediaOctober 14, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 14, 2024</span></span></span>

## The reported scalp biopsy finding

The strongest published argument for a non-radiological explanation comes from the reported scalp biopsy.

Schuessler's account states that pathological examination of Cash's scalp tissue supported a diagnosis of **alopecia areata**, and later commentators have highlighted this as one of the most significant medical findings in the surviving record. Gary Posner, summarising the same material, notes that the biopsy was interpreted as supporting alopecia areata, although he also records that radiation advocate Dr Robert Rank disputed the pathologist's interpretation. The disagreement therefore concerns how the biopsy should be interpreted, not whether the biopsy itself was reportedly performed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10581488/files/Reliability_I-12_Posner.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">I. Case Studies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">I. Case StudiesJune 16, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 16, 2025</span></span></span>

Modern dermatology recognises scalp biopsy as an accepted investigation when the cause of hair loss is uncertain. While many cases of alopecia areata are diagnosed clinically, biopsy can help distinguish it from other causes of alopecia by demonstrating characteristic inflammatory changes around actively growing hair follicles. Current dermatological guidance therefore makes the reported biopsy particularly relevant to retrospective discussions of Cash's diagnosis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537000/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIAlopecia Areata</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">StatPearls - NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

## Radiation epilation versus alopecia areata

The two principal explanations imply different biological mechanisms.

Radiation epilation occurs when sufficiently high radiation doses damage rapidly dividing cells within hair follicles. Hair loss may appear after a latent period and is usually associated with significant local radiation exposure. Depending on dose, regrowth may occur or permanent loss may result.

Alopecia areata, by contrast, is an autoimmune disease in which immune cells attack otherwise healthy hair follicles. The follicles are generally not destroyed, making the condition a **non-scarring** form of hair loss with the potential for later regrowth. It commonly presents as sharply defined patches but can occasionally become more diffuse, particularly in rapidly evolving cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537000/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIAlopecia Areata</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">StatPearls - NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

Several distinctions are relevant when comparing these mechanisms in Cash's case:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Underlying cause:** radiation epilation reflects direct tissue injury, whereas alopecia areata results from immune-mediated inflammation.
* **Diagnostic approach:** radiation injury is established primarily through exposure history, dose estimation and associated clinical findings, while alopecia areata is diagnosed from characteristic clinical features and, when necessary, scalp biopsy.
* **Associated features:** alopecia areata typically produces smooth, non-scarring areas of hair loss, whereas severe radiation injury is often accompanied by broader evidence of radiation damage to affected skin and tissues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537000/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIAlopecia Areata</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">StatPearls - NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

</div>

These distinctions mean that hair loss alone is insufficient evidence for either diagnosis.

## Why the biopsy remains important

The reported biopsy has become one of the pivotal pieces of medical evidence because it provides a recognised diagnosis that does not depend upon an extraordinary exposure.

Importantly, this does not establish that every symptom Cash experienced is explained by alopecia areata. Her admissions involved facial swelling, scalp tenderness, crusting and other dermatological problems beyond hair loss. The biopsy instead addresses a narrower question: whether the later alopecia necessarily demonstrates radiation injury.

Because alopecia areata is a well-established autoimmune disorder that can be confirmed histologically, the reported biopsy substantially weakens the argument that Cash's hair loss alone constitutes proof of radiation exposure. At the same time, disagreement over the biopsy's interpretation—particularly Dr Rank's criticism of the pathology reading—means it cannot be treated as an uncontested resolution of the broader medical debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10581488/files/Reliability_I-12_Posner.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">I. ## What this means for interpreting Cash's hair loss

Viewed in isolation, [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }})'s later alopecia cannot reliably distinguish between competing explanations. The chronology shows that marked hair loss appeared after her initial hospital admission rather than immediately following the reported encounter. The reported scalp biopsy then introduced a recognised autoimmune diagnosis—alopecia areata—that offered a medically conventional explanation for that specific symptom.

Consequently, the available medical record supports a more limited conclusion than is often presented in popular accounts. It documents genuine and significant hair loss, but it also documents evidence that clinicians considered an alternative diagnosis unrelated to radiation. Whether one accepts the biopsy interpretation or favours competing explanations, the existence of that reported pathological finding means Cash's alopecia cannot be regarded as unambiguous evidence of radiation-induced injury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10581488/files/Reliability_I-12_Posner.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[zenodo.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">I.
