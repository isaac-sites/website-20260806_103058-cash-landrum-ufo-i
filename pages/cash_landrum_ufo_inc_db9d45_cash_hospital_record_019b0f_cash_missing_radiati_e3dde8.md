## Introduction

A central weakness in Betty Cash's radiation claim is not simply that alternative medical explanations exist, but that the key tests normally used to establish significant ionising radiation exposure were either not performed, not reported publicly, or have never been produced. Her hospital records document that she became seriously ill after the alleged Cash–Landrum encounter, but proving radiation injury requires more than symptoms such as nausea, headache, skin changes or weakness because those features are medically non-specific. Modern radiation medicine relies on objective measurements—including [serial blood counts]({{ 'blood-counts-a88e65/' | relative_url }}), chromosome analysis and dose reconstruction—to determine whether a patient actually received a harmful [radiation dose]({{ 'radiation-dose/' | relative_url }}). The absence of those measurements means the medical evidence can demonstrate illness but cannot reliably quantify or confirm radiation exposure as its cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 23, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2024</span></span></span>

## Blood-count patterns expected after significant exposure

One of the most informative early tests after suspected whole-body radiation exposure is the pattern of lymphocyte depletion.

Radiation damages rapidly dividing tissues, including bone marrow. As a result, absolute lymphocyte counts typically begin falling within hours after sufficiently large exposures. Rather than relying on a single blood sample, clinicians obtain multiple CBCs over the first 8–12 hours and continue monitoring for several days. The rate of decline provides an estimate of absorbed dose and helps distinguish radiation injury from illnesses with similar symptoms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 23, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2024</span></span></span>

If exposure is substantial enough to produce true acute radiation syndrome, clinicians also expect characteristic changes over time in:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* total white blood cell counts;
* neutrophils;
* platelets; and
* later evidence of bone marrow suppression.

</div>

Publicly available accounts of [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }})'s [medical records]({{ 'medical-record/' | relative_url }}) do not include serial lymphocyte measurements or the characteristic blood-count curves that modern radiation medicine would expect when evaluating suspected ARS. Individual laboratory values may have been obtained during hospital care, but no published record has demonstrated the sequential pattern required to estimate radiation dose or to support a diagnosis of acute radiation syndrome. This absence does not prove radiation was impossible; it means one of the strongest objective diagnostic tools is unavailable for independent evaluation.

## Why chromosome testing matters

Even if early blood-count data are incomplete, radiation medicine has another important tool: cytogenetic biodosimetry.

The best-established method is the dicentric chromosome assay, which examines peripheral blood lymphocytes for a specific type of chromosome abnormality created by ionising radiation. Because spontaneous dicentric chromosomes are extremely uncommon, their frequency provides a biological estimate of radiation dose and has become the international reference standard for retrospective dose assessment after accidental exposures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nucleus.iaea.org/sites/nss-oui/Published%20Chunks/m_cf1f9f91-f97c-4467-a9e6-e24560302f61/c_465a704d-72b5-4f55-be33-42757d378fb3__1_0.Html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nucleus.iaea.org">[Nucleus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nucleus.iaea.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nucleus GSG-7 Occupational Radiation Protection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GSG-7 Occupational Radiation Protection - Annex: Techniques for Retrospective Dosimetry - Cytogenetic Techniques - Dicentric chrom...</span></span></span>

The test is particularly valuable when:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* no personal radiation dosimeter was worn;
* physical measurements are unavailable; or
* exposure is disputed.

</div>

International Atomic Energy Agency guidance describes the dicentric assay as the "gold standard" of biological dosimetry for acute radiation accidents, capable of estimating clinically significant whole-body doses when blood samples are collected within an appropriate timeframe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.iaea.org/sites/default/files/21/12/12_accident-_reformat.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iaea.org">[IAEA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iaea.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Assessment of Occupational Exposure due</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Assessment of Occupational Exposure due...</span></span></span>

No publicly available evidence indicates that Betty Cash underwent chromosome biodosimetry during or shortly after her illness. Without such testing, there is no biological dose estimate that can independently support or refute claims of substantial ionising radiation exposure.

## The missing physical radiation evidence

Medical findings are only one side of radiation investigations. Radiation accidents are also investigated through environmental and physical measurements.

Investigators would normally seek evidence such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* radiation surveys of the reported location;
* contamination measurements on vehicles or clothing;
* instrument readings from emergency responders;
* measurements of radioactive materials; or
* independent reconstruction of the exposure geometry and likely absorbed dose.

</div>

In the Cash–Landrum case, no publicly documented radiation survey has established hazardous radiation levels at the scene, and no accepted absorbed-dose reconstruction has been published. Consequently, there is no physical dosimetry that can be compared with the reported medical symptoms.

This distinction is important because a diagnosis of radiation injury becomes substantially stronger when biological findings and environmental measurements independently point to the same level of exposure. In Cash's case, neither line of objective evidence has been publicly established.

## Why symptoms alone cannot establish radiation illness

Several symptoms reported by Betty Cash—including nausea, headache, weakness, skin irritation and later [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }})—can occur after significant radiation exposure. However, each can also arise from numerous infectious, inflammatory, autoimmune or toxic conditions.

For that reason, modern clinical guidance specifically warns that acute radiation syndrome cannot be diagnosed from symptoms alone. Instead, unexplained symptoms should prompt laboratory testing, serial blood counts, chromosome analysis and consultation with radiation specialists before radiation injury is confirmed or excluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 23, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2024</span></span></span>

This principle explains why debates over the Cash–Landrum incident continue. Supporters often emphasise the similarity between Cash's symptoms and radiation illness, while sceptics note that resemblance alone cannot substitute for objective dosimetry or characteristic laboratory findings.

## Why the missing tests limit the claim

The absence of key radiation-specific investigations does not demonstrate that Betty Cash was never exposed to radiation. Medical records can be incomplete, and some specialised biodosimetry techniques were less readily available in routine hospital practice in the early 1980s than they are today.

However, the missing evidence has an important consequence: it prevents the claim from reaching the evidential standard normally expected for documenting a significant accidental radiation exposure.

Specifically, there is no publicly available record of:

* serial lymphocyte depletion used to estimate radiation dose;
* chromosome biodosimetry confirming radiation-induced DNA damage;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://researchportal.ukhsa.gov.uk/en/publications/radiation-induced-chromosome-damage-in-human-lymphocytes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportal.ukhsa.gov.uk">[researchportal.ukhsa.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportal.ukhsa.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ukhsa.gov.uk Radiation-induced chromosome damage in human lymphocytes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ukhsa.gov.uk Radiation-induced chromosome damage in human lymphocytes</span></span></span>
* physical radiation measurements from the scene or associated objects; or
* a reconstructed absorbed dose that ties the [reported symptoms]({{ 'reported-symptoms/' | relative_url }}) to a quantified exposure.

As a result, the available medical evidence supports the conclusion that Betty Cash experienced a serious illness requiring prolonged hospital treatment, but it does not independently establish that ionising radiation caused that illness. That distinction remains one of the most significant unresolved issues in evaluating the medical aspect of the Cash–Landrum incident.
