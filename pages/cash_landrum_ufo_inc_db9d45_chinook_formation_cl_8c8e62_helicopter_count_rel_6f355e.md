## Introduction

The reported [helicopter count]({{ 'helicopter-count/' | relative_url }}) is one of the most debated aspects of the Cash–Landrum incident because it sits at the intersection of eyewitness credibility and practical plausibility. Betty Cash consistently described seeing about 23 helicopters, while Vickie Landrum recalled approximately 26. Those figures have often been repeated as though they represented a precise tally, but the primary evidence suggests something more modest: both witnesses were attempting to estimate a very large number of aircraft under stressful, fast-moving night-time conditions. The exact figure is therefore uncertain, even if the broader claim—that they believed they saw an unusually large [helicopter formation]({{ 'helicopters-977f69/' | relative_url }})—remained central to their account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

## How Cash and Landrum arrived at different totals

The best evidence for the famous helicopter count comes from the witnesses' recorded interview at Bergstrom Air Force Base in August 1981, roughly eight months after the incident. During that interview, Betty Cash stated that she counted [23 helicopters]({{ '23-helicopters/' | relative_url }}), while also explaining that [Vickie Landrum]({{ 'vickie-landrum/' | relative_url }}) had arrived at a different total of about 26. Rather than presenting the numbers as exact, both women acknowledged that they were estimates made during an extraordinary event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

The interview transcript is revealing because investigators specifically questioned how such a count had been made at night. Landrum replied that she had been counting aloud as helicopters continued arriving and moving through the area. She added that some were large tandem-rotor aircraft while others appeared to be smaller conventional helicopters. Her explanation indicates an ongoing estimate rather than a single moment when every aircraft was simultaneously visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

This distinction is important because later retellings often simplify the story into "exactly 23 Chinook helicopters". The primary interview supports a more cautious interpretation: the witnesses believed they observed roughly two dozen helicopters, with only some identified by their distinctive tandem-rotor configuration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

## Why counting aircraft at night is inherently difficult

Several factors reduce confidence in the numerical precision without necessarily invalidating the broader observation.

First, the event unfolded after dark. Even large helicopters can be difficult to distinguish individually when viewed mainly by navigation lights, silhouettes and rotor noise. If aircraft cross paths or orbit a common point, separating one helicopter from another becomes increasingly difficult.

Second, the witnesses were not stationary observers making a careful inventory. Their vehicle was moving for part of the encounter, while the reported object and helicopters were also changing position. Relative motion creates repeated viewing angles that increase the possibility of counting the same aircraft more than once.

Third, the witnesses themselves described being frightened and distracted. [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }}) had reportedly been exposed to intense heat moments earlier, and all three occupants were focused primarily on the unusual object rather than conducting an organised count of surrounding aircraft. Cognitive research on eyewitness perception consistently shows that stress, divided attention and rapidly changing scenes reduce the accuracy of numerical estimates, even when the central event is remembered vividly.

None of these factors prove that the witnesses exaggerated the number deliberately. Instead, they illustrate why an estimate made under such circumstances should be expected to contain a significant margin of error.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

## Does the disagreement between 23 and 26 matter?

In practice, the difference between 23 and 26 is relatively small. Both estimates describe essentially the same phenomenon: an exceptionally large helicopter presence.

If the witnesses had independently reported dramatically different numbers—for example, five helicopters versus thirty—the discrepancy would raise more serious concerns. Instead, their estimates differ by only three aircraft, well within the range expected when multiple people attempt to estimate a large moving group under poor viewing conditions.

That consistency cuts both ways. It lends some support to the claim that they believed they were observing a substantial formation, but it does not establish that either total was objectively correct.

## What the number proves—even if it was imprecise

The evidential value of the helicopter count lies less in whether there were exactly 23 aircraft than in what such a claim would imply if substantially accurate.

A formation approaching two dozen heavy military helicopters would represent a major aviation operation rather than an isolated flight. Such an operation would normally involve multiple crews, maintenance support, fuel planning, communications and operational records. For that reason, Army investigators focused heavily on tracing possible helicopter activity.

The subsequent Army Inspector General inquiry contacted numerous military organisations, National Guard units, reserve aviation units, Fort Hood, Ellington facilities and other relevant commands. According to the memorandum, investigators found no records placing Army, Army National Guard or Army Reserve helicopters in the reported area during the relevant period, despite specifically examining the possibility because the witnesses described about 23 helicopters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/cashlandrumsarran01.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: the Cash-Landrum case, 1980, USAF memo 1/4, Sarran...</span></span></span>

This does not demonstrate that the witnesses fabricated the count. Equally, it means the reported number has never been independently verified through operational documentation. ## The most balanced assessment

The reported helicopter count should be understood as an approximate eyewitness estimate rather than a verified inventory.

The primary sources support several conclusions simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The witnesses consistently maintained that they saw an unusually large number of helicopters.
* Their own estimates differed slightly, suggesting they were approximations rather than precise counts.
* Night-time conditions, moving aircraft and acute stress make exact counting inherently unreliable.
* No independent military documentation has confirmed that roughly two dozen helicopters were present.

</div>

Taken together, the evidence supports confidence in only the broadest proposition: Cash and Landrum believed they witnessed a very large helicopter formation. Whether that formation contained exactly 23 helicopters, somewhat fewer, or somewhat more cannot be determined from the available evidence, and the surviving documentation does not justify treating the famous figure as an established fact.
