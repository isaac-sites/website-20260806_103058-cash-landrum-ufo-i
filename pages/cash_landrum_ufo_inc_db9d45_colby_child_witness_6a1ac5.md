## His reported view from the car

Colby’s position during the encounter differed from that of the adults. Cash reportedly left the vehicle and remained outside longer, while Landrum briefly emerged before returning to the car because Colby was frightened. In the August 1981 interview at Bergstrom Air Force Base, Landrum described holding him inside the doorway and trying to distract or reassure him while the object remained visible. Her account places him close to the spectacle but partly shielded by the car and by an adult who was actively managing his attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/CashLandrum1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO CasebookCash/Landrum Interview, Part 1Cash/Landrum Interview, Part 1 TRANSCRIPT OF BERGSTROM AFB, INTERVIEW OF BETTY CASH, VICKIE &amp; C...</span></span></span>

That distinction is important. Colby was not observing calmly under controlled conditions; he was watching an intensely bright, apparently hot object while distressed adults moved around him. He may have had a clear view of certain features but only fragmentary awareness of the sequence as a whole. A frightening witness can remember a striking central image while remaining uncertain about timing, distance and peripheral details. Research on acute stress and eyewitness memory does not support a simple rule that fear either preserves or destroys recall; its effects vary with the event, the level of stress and the kind of information being remembered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34309476/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The object’s diamond shape is the clearest example of Colby’s distinctive contribution. Later case accounts usually treat the shape as a shared observation, but summaries of the witnesses’ first recorded report state that Cash and Landrum could not clearly discern it through the glare, whereas Colby maintained that it resembled a large diamond. By the Bergstrom interview eight months later, the adults also described and sketched a diamond-like object. This sequence does not show that Colby invented the shape; it shows that the familiar consolidated description may have developed through interaction among the three witnesses rather than through three fully independent observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incidentThe Cash–Landrum Incident was an unidentified flying object sighting in the United States in 1980, which witnesses c...</span></span></span>

He said he did not feel ill until the following morning, when his stomach hurt and he experienced diarrhoea. He also reported a small patch of [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}), later blisters and eye irritation in sunlight or swimming pools. In a much later interview discussed in 2013, the adult Colby described twin-rotor helicopters surrounding the object at a distance and said the scene remained vivid in his mind. ## Fear and family influence

Colby’s childhood experience was shaped not only by what he saw but by how the adults around him interpreted it. Landrum later said she tried to calm him by describing the brilliant presence in religious terms and telling him to look for Jesus inside it. The remark reveals an immediate attempt to convert an incomprehensible and frightening event into something familiar. It does not establish that Colby adopted that explanation, but it shows that his perception was accompanied from the outset by strong adult framing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/CashLandrum1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO CasebookCash/Landrum Interview, Part 1Cash/Landrum Interview, Part 1 TRANSCRIPT OF BERGSTROM AFB, INTERVIEW OF BETTY CASH, VICKIE &amp; C...</span></span></span>

This matters because young witnesses do not encode an event in isolation from the people protecting them. At seven, Colby was living under his grandmother’s legal custody and depended on her for safety, explanation and later medical care. During the encounter she reportedly held him in the car; afterwards she spoke for him frequently in interviews, described his symptoms and interpreted the object as a dangerous human-made vehicle associated with military helicopters. Her concern may have been entirely sincere, but the family relationship makes it difficult to separate what Colby independently remembered from what became the household’s shared understanding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Computer UFO Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cufon.org.</span></span></span>

A particularly revealing episode occurred when a CH-47 Chinook helicopter visited Dayton several months after the incident. Published case summaries report that Colby became highly upset on seeing it, prompting Landrum to take him closer to the landed aircraft in an effort to reduce his fear. The reaction is consistent with a child who associated the distinctive twin-rotor shape or sound with a terrifying night. It supports the conclusion that the experience had emotional force for him, but it cannot determine whether every remembered helicopter detail was accurate. Fear can be genuine even when the precise source, sequence or interpretation of a memory remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incidentThe Cash–Landrum Incident was an unidentified flying object sighting in the United States in 1980, which witnesses c...</span></span></span>

The adults’ subsequent health anxieties may also have deepened that fear. In the Bergstrom interview, Landrum discussed warnings she said she had received about possible future illness in Colby, including the possibility of a leukaemia-like condition. She also described treating him largely at home because of financial pressure. Hearing such predictions while the family pursued medical explanations and government responsibility could have made the incident an ongoing threat rather than a single frightening memory. The transcript records the family’s anxiety, but it does not supply clinical evidence that Colby faced the specific long-term risk described.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blueblurrylines.com/2013/07/from-their-own-lips-betty-cash-colby.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blueblurrylines.com">[Blue Blurry Lines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blueblurrylines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blue Blurry LinesBlue Blurry Lines: From their own lips: Betty, Colby &amp; Vickie tell their storyJuly 23, 2013 — full Bergstrom interview t...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 23, 2013</span></span></span>

Modern memory research provides a reason for caution without implying deception. Studies show that children can absorb information from co-witnesses through both social agreement and actual memory change. In one study, most co-witness-related errors were attributed to memory distortion rather than deliberate conformity; research involving children aged six and seven has likewise found that discussion with another witness can affect later reports. Colby’s circumstances—three witnesses travelling together, repeated family discussion and interviews conducted months afterwards—contained several conditions in which memories could converge naturally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22715814/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">By separating errors made in public and private, the results indicated that both social conformity (32% of errors) and memory distortion...</span></span></span>

That convergence cuts both ways. Agreement between Colby and the adults may reflect a shared external event, shared discussion afterwards, or a mixture of both. Disagreement can also be informative: his early insistence on a diamond shape, when the adults were less definite, suggests that his account was not simply a word-for-word repetition of theirs. The evidential challenge is that no preserved, immediate and separately conducted interview allows investigators to establish which details originated independently with each witness.

## The value and limits of child testimony

Colby’s account has genuine evidential value because it adds a third observer with a different physical and emotional perspective. He was inside or at the edge of the car for much of the encounter, focused on safety and close to Landrum, whereas Cash spent longer outside. His recollection of the object’s shape, his apparent fear of helicopters and his later willingness to say when he did not remember something give his testimony a recognisable personal texture rather than the appearance of a wholly scripted account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.whipnet.com/sites/ufo.whipnet.org/xdocs/Cash.Landrum/trans1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whipnet.com">[Whipnet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whipnet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO.Whipnet.org &#124; Aliens &#124; UFO &#124; The Piney Woods UFO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aliens &#124; UFO &#124; The Piney Woods UFO - Transcript 1UFO.Whipnet.org &#124; Aliens &#124; UFO &#124; The Piney Woods UFO - Transcript 1 Documents Photos R...</span></span></span>

The strongest use of his evidence is therefore corroborative but limited. It supports the proposition that the family experienced something they regarded as visually extraordinary and frightening, and that Colby associated both a fiery object and twin-rotor helicopters with the episode. It does not independently establish the object’s size, exact distance, ownership or physical effects. Nor can his [reported symptoms]({{ 'reported-symptoms/' | relative_url }}) prove radiation exposure without contemporaneous clinical measurements, a verified dose or a diagnosis excluding other causes.

Interview quality is central to that distinction. Best-practice child-witness protocols emphasise rapport, clear ground rules, free recall and open-ended questions, because repeated, suggestive or assumption-laden questioning can increase error. The surviving Bergstrom transcript is valuable because it preserves some of Colby’s own words, but it was recorded roughly eight months after the encounter and within a joint session attended by both adult witnesses. Much of the narrative was supplied before Colby was questioned directly, reducing the independence that a modern forensic interview would seek to protect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/understanding-rapport-building-investigative-interviews-does-rapports-effect-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nij.ojp.gov">[ojp.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nij.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

The later adult interview presents a different evidential problem. Colby’s continuing conviction and emotional response show that the event remained significant throughout his life. His ability to identify remembered features decades later may preserve authentic childhood impressions, especially broad features such as intense light, flame, heat or double-rotor helicopters. But later confidence cannot reveal how much of a memory was retained continuously and how much was reconstructed through photographs, drawings, family narratives and public treatments of the incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blueblurrylines.com/2013/12/more-details-from-ufo-witness-colby.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blueblurrylines.com">[Blue Blurry Lines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blueblurrylines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blueblurrylines.com.</span></span></span>

For that reason, Colby should not be labelled either the case’s decisive witness or its weak link. Treating a seven-year-old as inherently unreliable would ignore evidence that children can give accurate, meaningful accounts, especially when allowed to report freely. Treating his recollections as independent confirmation of every adult claim would ignore the effects of fear, delay, shared discussion and caregiver influence. Research reviews on child witnesses repeatedly reach the more measured conclusion: reliability depends heavily on how the child experienced the event and how the testimony was elicited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/eye-witness-testimony-children" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ojp.gov.</span></span></span>

Colby Landrum’s childhood testimony is most persuasive at its simplest. A frightened boy said he saw a brilliant, diamond-like object and military-style helicopters during an event that deeply alarmed the adults caring for him. The records show that this was a lasting and emotionally consequential memory. They do not provide the clean separation, immediate interviewing or physical corroboration needed to determine which details came directly from his original perception and which were strengthened, altered or organised within the family’s later account.
