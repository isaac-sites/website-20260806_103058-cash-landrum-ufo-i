## Introduction

Before Betty Cash, [Vickie Landrum]({{ 'vickie-landrum/' | relative_url }}) and Colby Landrum could sue the United States over the 1980 [Cash–Landrum UFO incident]({{ 'cash-landrum-ufo-incident/' | relative_url }}), federal law required them to present their claims to the responsible government agency. That administrative stage was more than a procedural formality. The Air Force's final denial both opened the courthouse door under the Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA) and revealed the central weakness that would shape every later litigation decision: the government disputed that any federal personnel, aircraft or equipment were involved at all. Rather than resolving factual questions about the alleged encounter, the denial established the legal battlefield. The plaintiffs could proceed to court, but they would have to overcome the government's explicit position that no military connection existed—a burden that ultimately proved decisive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/2675" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Legal Information Institute28 U.S. Code § 2675 - Disposition by federal agency as prerequisite; evidence &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Leg...</span></span></span>

## Why an administrative claim was required

The FTCA is a limited waiver of the United States' sovereign immunity. Congress required claimants to give the relevant federal agency the first opportunity to investigate and, where appropriate, settle claims before litigation begins. Under 28 U.S.C. § 2675(a), a lawsuit generally cannot be filed until the claimant either receives a written final denial or the agency fails to act for six months.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/2675" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Legal Information Institute28 U.S. Code § 2675 - Disposition by federal agency as prerequisite; evidence &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Leg...</span></span></span>

For the Cash–Landrum claimants, this meant presenting their allegations to the Air Force before commencing an FTCA action. At that stage, they were effectively asking the government to investigate whether federal personnel or equipment had caused their alleged injuries and whether compensation was warranted.

This administrative process served two distinct purposes:

* it satisfied a mandatory jurisdictional prerequisite for an FTCA suit;
* it allowed the Air Force to conduct its own factual investigation before any court became involved.

Importantly, completing the administrative process did not shift the burden of proof. Even after exhausting the statutory requirement, the plaintiffs still had to prove in court that a federal employee or instrumentality was legally responsible for their injuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/2675" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Legal Information Institute28 U.S. Code § 2675 - Disposition by federal agency as prerequisite; evidence &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Leg...</span></span></span>

## What the Air Force's final denial did—and did not—decide

The Air Force's letter of 2 September 1983 became one of the most important procedural documents in the case. It rejected the personal injury claims after concluding that the alleged facts did not establish responsibility by the United States or any of its agencies. According to the letter, the government's investigation had uncovered no evidence that military personnel, aircraft or equipment were involved in the incident. The letter also stated that acceptance and processing of the claim should not be interpreted as admitting the truth of the witnesses' allegations. Finally, it advised that the denial constituted the agency's final administrative action and satisfied the FTCA's exhaustion requirement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electronicsandbooks.com/edt/manual/Magazine/M/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal%20-%20September%201983.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electronicsandbooks.com">[Electronics and Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electronicsandbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronics and Books MUFON UFO JOURNAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronics and Books MUFON UFO JOURNAL</span></span></span>

Legally, the denial had carefully limited significance.

It **did decide** that the Air Force would not voluntarily compensate the claimants through the [administrative claims]({{ 'claims-process/' | relative_url }}) process.

It **did not decide** that the witnesses had fabricated their experience or that no unusual event had occurred. Nor did it represent a judicial determination of the disputed facts. Instead, it reflected the agency's conclusion that its investigation found no evidence connecting the alleged incident to the federal government.

That distinction became critical in later litigation. The plaintiffs remained free to argue that the Air Force investigation was incomplete or mistaken. However, because the government had already taken the position that no military involvement existed, the plaintiffs entered court knowing precisely which factual issue would dominate the case.

## How the denial shaped the litigation strategy

Rather than changing the plaintiffs' basic theory, the administrative denial sharpened its focus.

If the Air Force denied ownership or participation, the plaintiffs would have to produce independent evidence linking the incident to the federal government. That strategic necessity elevated the reported military-style helicopters from an interesting detail to the cornerstone of the FTCA case.

The litigation therefore concentrated on questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* whether the helicopters actually belonged to the United States;
* whether they were operating under federal authority;
* whether they were escorting or controlling the unidentified object;
* whether documentary evidence, flight records or witness testimony could establish federal responsibility despite the Air Force's denial.

</div>

The administrative denial effectively previewed the government's courtroom defence. Rather than disputing only the extent of the claimed injuries, the United States contested the threshold issue of ownership and involvement. Once that position was formalised in writing, the plaintiffs knew they would need evidence stronger than eyewitness impressions of military-looking aircraft.

The denial also highlighted the evidentiary gap that would become increasingly significant during discovery. Without identifiable aircraft numbers, operational records, named crews or other corroborating documentation, the plaintiffs faced the difficult task of proving federal involvement largely through circumstantial evidence while the government relied on [sworn denials]({{ 'sworn-denials/' | relative_url }}) and the absence of official records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electronicsandbooks.com/edt/manual/Magazine/M/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal%20-%20September%201983.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electronicsandbooks.com">[Electronics and Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electronicsandbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronics and Books MUFON UFO JOURNAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronics and Books MUFON UFO JOURNAL</span></span></span>

## How the six-month filing window affected the lawsuit

The denial letter did more than reject the claim. It also informed the claimants that they had six months from the mailing of the letter to file suit in federal court under the FTCA. That deadline arose from the Act's statute of limitations and marked the transition from the administrative process to judicial proceedings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electronicsandbooks.com/edt/manual/Magazine/M/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal%20-%20September%201983.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electronicsandbooks.com">[Electronics and Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electronicsandbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronics and Books MUFON UFO JOURNAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronics and Books MUFON UFO JOURNAL</span></span></span>

This timing had several practical consequences.

First, the plaintiffs could no longer wait indefinitely in the hope that additional government investigations would produce new evidence. They had to decide whether the evidence already assembled justified filing suit before the statutory window closed.

Second, because the administrative process had ended without a settlement, litigation became the only remaining avenue for seeking damages from the federal government.

Finally, once the complaint was filed, the focus shifted from persuading an agency to reconsider its position to satisfying federal rules of evidence and civil procedure. The administrative record could provide context, but it did not substitute for admissible proof establishing the government's liability.

## The administrative stage foreshadowed the case's ultimate weakness

In retrospect, the administrative proceedings accurately anticipated the central issue that later defeated the FTCA action.

The Air Force's denial did not merely reject compensation; it identified the precise factual dispute that the plaintiffs would have to overcome in court. Their legal theory depended on proving that federal personnel or equipment were connected to the incident. The agency's investigation reported no such evidence, forcing the plaintiffs to rely on independent proof that ultimately proved insufficient to create a legally sustainable dispute over government responsibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electronicsandbooks.com/edt/manual/Magazine/M/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal/MUFON%20UFO%20Journal%20-%20September%201983.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electronicsandbooks.com">[Electronics and Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electronicsandbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electronics and Books MUFON UFO JOURNAL</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronics and Books MUFON UFO JOURNAL</span></span></span>

For that reason, the administrative claim occupies an important place in the history of the Cash–Landrum litigation. It was both the procedural gateway required by the FTCA and an early indication that the decisive issue would not be whether something extraordinary had occurred on a Texas road, but whether that event could be legally attributed to the United States government.
