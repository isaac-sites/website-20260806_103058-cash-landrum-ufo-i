## Introduction

In the Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA) litigation arising from the Cash–Landrum incident, the plaintiffs' [medical records]({{ 'medical-record/' | relative_url }}) were important but legally incomplete. They documented that Betty Cash, [Vickie Landrum]({{ 'vickie-landrum/' | relative_url }}) and Colby Landrum sought medical treatment for genuine symptoms after the reported encounter, yet those records could not identify what physical agent caused those symptoms, when and how any exposure occurred, or who was legally responsible for it. That distinction was crucial. Under the FTCA, proving that an illness existed was only one part of the evidential chain; the plaintiffs also had to prove that the illness was caused by the negligent conduct of an identifiable federal actor. The medical evidence established treatment and documented symptoms, but it did not establish the exposure source or connect that source to the United States government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

## What the treatment records could establish

The hospital and physician records served several legitimate evidential purposes. They showed that the witnesses did not simply describe symptoms years later from memory. [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }}), in particular, received extensive medical care, including hospitalisation, and the records documented burns, blistering, nausea, weakness, [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}) and other medical problems that developed after the reported event. Later examinations continued to record persistent health complaints.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anomalydesk.com/cash-landrum-incident.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anomalydesk.com">[AnomalyDesk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anomalydesk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cash landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cash-Landrum Incident (December 1980) &#124; AnomalyDesk Case File 088May 21, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 21, 2026</span></span></span>

Some treating physicians considered parts of the clinical picture compatible with significant thermal or radiation-type injury. That medical opinion helped demonstrate that the symptoms deserved serious investigation rather than dismissal as imaginary. However, describing a presentation as *consistent with* radiation exposure is not the same as proving that ionising radiation actually caused it. Medical diagnosis often works by considering multiple possible explanations, and clinical similarity alone cannot identify a unique exposure source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anomalydesk.com/cash-landrum-incident.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anomalydesk.com">[AnomalyDesk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anomalydesk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cash landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cash-Landrum Incident (December 1980) &#124; AnomalyDesk Case File 088May 21, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 21, 2026</span></span></span>

From a litigation perspective, these records therefore established three limited propositions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the plaintiffs genuinely sought medical treatment;
* physicians documented objective and subjective symptoms;
* the illnesses followed the reported encounter chronologically.

</div>

None of those propositions answered the separate legal question of what caused the illnesses.

## What exposure evidence was missing

The central weakness was not the existence of illness but the absence of reliable evidence identifying the physical mechanism behind it.

If ionising radiation had been the alleged cause, investigators would ideally have needed evidence such as environmental radiation measurements, contamination of clothing or vehicles, dosimetry data, identifiable radioactive materials, or laboratory findings capable of quantifying a specific exposure. None of those forms of evidence was available from the reported incident. Later commentary has noted that some laboratory investigations were inconclusive rather than definitive, leaving no objective measurement that could establish either the dose or the source of any radiation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://anomalydesk.com/cash-landrum-incident.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: anomalydesk.com">[AnomalyDesk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">anomalydesk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cash landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cash-Landrum Incident (December 1980) &#124; AnomalyDesk Case File 088May 21, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 21, 2026</span></span></span>

Equally important, the medical records themselves could not answer several essential forensic questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was the injury caused by ionising radiation, intense heat, chemicals, infection or another process?
* If radiation was involved, what isotope or energy source produced it?
* What was the absorbed dose?
* Where did the exposure occur?
* Who controlled the source?

</div>

Hospital charts are designed primarily to diagnose and treat patients, not to reconstruct the origin of an unknown environmental exposure. Unless an exposure source is independently identified, clinicians generally record symptoms and possible diagnoses rather than assigning legal responsibility.

## Why diagnosis could not establish legal causation

Medical causation and legal causation are related but distinct concepts.

A physician may conclude that a patient's symptoms appear compatible with a particular type of injury without being able to identify the exact event that produced it. Courts, however, require a more complete evidential chain when assigning liability.

In the Cash–Landrum litigation, the plaintiffs needed evidence linking four separate propositions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. a specific hazardous exposure occurred;
2. that exposure caused the medical injuries;
3. the hazardous source belonged to or was controlled by the federal government;
4. federal negligence caused the exposure.

</div>

The medical records addressed only part of the second proposition. They did not establish the first, third or fourth. Even if every symptom had been accepted as genuine, the records alone could not identify the responsible instrumentality or demonstrate [federal control]({{ 'federal-control/' | relative_url }}) over it. This evidential gap became particularly significant after government agencies denied that military aircraft or personnel were involved in the incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

## How alternative causes weakened the legal chain

Another reason the medical evidence proved insufficient was that the observed symptoms were not unique to one cause.

Burns, nausea, fatigue, eye irritation, hair loss and immune-related problems can arise from numerous medical conditions or environmental exposures. Later medical reviews and sceptical analyses questioned whether some symptoms could have reflected pre-existing illness or alternative diagnoses rather than acute radiation injury. For example, discussion has focused on Betty Cash's prior medical history and on whether some later hair loss could have resulted from [alopecia areata]({{ 'alopecia-areata/' | relative_url }}) rather than radiation damage. These competing explanations remain disputed, but their legal significance was straightforward: if reasonable alternative causes existed, the plaintiffs bore the burden of excluding them sufficiently to establish causation on the balance required by law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

This does not mean the plaintiffs were required to eliminate every conceivable alternative explanation. Rather, they needed evidence showing that the government's alleged conduct was the probable cause of the injuries. Medical records documenting illness, without independent proof identifying the exposure mechanism, left substantial room for competing interpretations.

## The gap between chronology and proof

One of the strongest features of the plaintiffs' case was temporal proximity: the [reported symptoms]({{ 'reported-symptoms/' | relative_url }}) appeared soon after the alleged encounter.

Chronology can support an inference of causation, especially when symptoms develop immediately after a significant event. Yet courts generally regard timing as supporting evidence rather than conclusive proof. A sequence of "event followed by illness" does not, by itself, establish that the event caused the illness.

That limitation was especially important here because the alleged exposure source itself remained unidentified. Without independently verified evidence showing what the witnesses encountered, the medical timeline could demonstrate coincidence in time but not necessarily causation in law.

## Why real illness was not enough to prove causation

The medical documentation strengthened the credibility of the claim that the witnesses experienced serious health problems. It did not, however, answer the questions that ultimately controlled the FTCA lawsuit.

The treatment records could not identify the physical agent responsible for the injuries, quantify any alleged exposure, distinguish conclusively between competing medical explanations, or connect the source of the exposure to an identifiable federal employee or government-controlled equipment. As a result, the plaintiffs possessed evidence of illness but lacked evidence completing the legal chain from symptoms to a specific negligent federal act.

That distinction illustrates an important principle of tort litigation: authentic medical injury does not by itself establish liability. Courts require reliable evidence linking the injury to the defendant's conduct, and in the Cash–Landrum case the medical records, however significant clinically, could not supply that missing link.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>
