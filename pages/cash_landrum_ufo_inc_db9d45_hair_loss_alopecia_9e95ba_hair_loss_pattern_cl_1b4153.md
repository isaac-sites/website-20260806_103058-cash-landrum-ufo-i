## Introduction

Within the Cash–Landrum incident, the *pattern* of [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }})'s reported [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}) is potentially more informative than the simple fact that hair was lost. In radiation medicine, clinicians assess not only when hair falls out but also where it occurs, whether it is sharply localised or diffuse, whether the scalp itself shows radiation injury, and how the hair later regrows. Those features help distinguish radiation-induced epilation from autoimmune alopecia, stress-related shedding and other causes. In the Cash–Landrum case, however, the publicly available record contains very little detailed description of the scalp findings. That absence is one of the main reasons the hair-loss evidence remains disputed: the feature that could have been most diagnostically useful was never documented in sufficient detail for independent assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/hair-disorders/alopecia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: merckmanuals.com">[merckmanuals.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">merckmanuals.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Merck Manuals Alopecia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Merck ManualsAlopecia - Dermatology - Merck Manual Professional Edition...</span></span></span>

## Patchy Versus Diffuse Shedding

The distinction between patchy and diffuse hair loss lies at the centre of the radiation debate.

Radiation exposure to a limited area of the scalp commonly produces localised epilation that reflects the geometry of the exposure. Modern examples include fluoroscopy-related radiation injuries, where patients often develop sharply bordered rectangular or otherwise well-defined bald patches corresponding to the radiation field. In therapeutic radiotherapy, hair loss similarly tends to mirror the treated area unless the entire scalp receives radiation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5198088/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Radiation-Induced Alopecia after Endovascular Embolization under Fluoroscopy - PMCDecember 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2016</span></span></span>

By contrast, diffuse shedding across most of the scalp has a much broader differential diagnosis. It may occur after systemic illness, severe physiological stress, chemotherapy, whole-scalp irradiation or certain autoimmune disorders. Diffuse loss therefore carries much less diagnostic specificity than a sharply localised radiation field.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/hair-disorders/alopecia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: merckmanuals.com">[Merck Manuals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">merckmanuals.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Merck Manuals Alopecia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Merck ManualsAlopecia - Dermatology - Merck Manual Professional Edition...</span></span></span>

The difficulty in the Cash–Landrum case is that publicly available medical discussions do not provide a detailed map of where the alopecia occurred. Later retellings often describe "clumps" of hair falling out, but losing hair in clumps does not itself indicate whether the resulting baldness was patchy or diffuse. Without contemporaneous scalp diagrams, dermatology photographs or specialist descriptions, the distribution cannot be reconstructed reliably.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

## Why Scalp Skin Findings Matter

Hair loss alone is only part of the diagnostic picture. Radiation capable of injuring hair follicles frequently affects nearby skin as well.

Clinicians evaluating cutaneous radiation injury look for findings such as:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/cri.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[cdc.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cutaneous Radiation Injury (CRI): Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDC...</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* erythema (redness) that develops after a latent period;
* blistering or ulceration within the irradiated region;
* pigmentation changes;
* loss of sweat or sebaceous gland function;
* later skin thinning or fibrosis; and
* permanent versus temporary follicle damage.

</div>

These associated findings help determine whether hair follicles were damaged by ionising radiation rather than by an unrelated process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/cri.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cutaneous Radiation Injury (CRI): Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDC...</span></span></span>

Cash unquestionably reported significant skin symptoms, including painful blistering, but the available public summaries do not clearly correlate those skin lesions with specific areas of hair loss. Nor do they include detailed dermatological descriptions of the scalp itself, such as whether bald regions showed scarring, ulceration, scaling, follicular destruction or otherwise normal-appearing skin. That missing correlation substantially limits retrospective diagnosis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

## What Regrowth Could Have Shown

The course of regrowth can also provide diagnostic clues.

Temporary [radiation epilation]({{ 'radiation-dose/' | relative_url }}) usually begins after a delay of several weeks and is followed by gradual regrowth over subsequent months if the follicle stem cells survive. Higher absorbed doses increase the likelihood of permanent alopecia because follicular structures are destroyed rather than temporarily arrested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5198088/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Radiation-Induced Alopecia after Endovascular Embolization under Fluoroscopy - PMCDecember 15, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 15, 2016</span></span></span>

Other causes of hair loss may show different recovery patterns. [Alopecia areata]({{ 'alopecia-areata/' | relative_url }}) often produces patchy regrowth with fine depigmented hairs before normal pigmentation returns, while stress-related telogen effluvium generally allows widespread regrowth once the triggering event resolves. These patterns are not individually diagnostic, but they contribute to an overall clinical assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/dermatologic-disorders/hair-disorders/alopecia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: merckmanuals.com">[Merck Manuals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">merckmanuals.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Merck Manuals Alopecia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Merck ManualsAlopecia - Dermatology - Merck Manual Professional Edition...</span></span></span>

In the Cash–Landrum case, publicly available accounts acknowledge substantial hair loss but provide only limited, sometimes inconsistent, information about its long-term regrowth. Because systematic follow-up dermatological examinations have not been published, later recovery cannot be used confidently either to support or to exclude radiation injury.

## Why the Pattern Evidence Remains Inconclusive

The debate persists not because hair loss lacks medical relevance, but because the most informative observations are largely absent from the public record.

Independent reviewers have noted that the available medical summaries do not include detailed scalp mapping, trichoscopy (magnified examination of hair follicles), scalp biopsy findings directed at the alopecia, or serial dermatology photography documenting how the pattern evolved. Later critiques have also highlighted that early hospital records reportedly documented little or no obvious hair loss at admission, while more pronounced alopecia developed later, leaving the distribution at its onset uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cash–Landrum incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident</span></span></span>

As a result, neither side of the radiation dispute can rely heavily on hair-loss pattern alone. Supporters of a radiation explanation can point to the existence of significant alopecia developing after the reported encounter, while sceptics note that the publicly documented pattern lacks the anatomical detail normally required to distinguish radiation injury from other recognised causes of hair loss. The absence of precise scalp findings therefore weakens the evidential value of one of the incident's most frequently cited medical features.
