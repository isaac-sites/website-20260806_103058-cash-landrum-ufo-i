## Introduction

One of the central medical arguments in the Cash–Landrum incident is whether [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }})'s reported [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}) is consistent with radiation-induced epilation. The answer is more nuanced than either supporters or sceptics often suggest. Radiation can indeed cause hair loss, and the reported delay before more substantial scalp hair loss became apparent is broadly compatible with the known biological response to a sufficiently large local radiation exposure. However, that timing alone does not establish radiation as the cause. Radiation epilation is a deterministic effect: it generally occurs only after the hair follicles receive a substantial absorbed dose, and no such dose was ever measured or reconstructed in Cash's case. The dispute therefore centres less on whether radiation *can* produce delayed hair loss than on whether there is evidence that the necessary scalp exposure actually occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.merckmanuals.com/en-ca/professional/multimedia/table/focal-radiation-injury" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: merckmanuals.com">[Merck Manuals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">merckmanuals.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Merck Manuals Table: Focal Radiation Injury*-Merck Manual Professional Edition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Merck Manuals Table: Focal Radiation Injury*-Merck Manual Professional Edition</span></span></span>

## Typical onset after local irradiation

Hair follicles are among the more radiation-sensitive tissues in the body, but they do not usually shed immediately after exposure. Ionising radiation damages the rapidly dividing cells within the hair follicle, and visible hair loss follows only after those injured follicles progress through their normal growth cycle.

Clinical references consistently place the onset of radiation-induced epilation approximately two to three weeks after a sufficiently high local exposure. The exact timing varies with absorbed dose, dose rate and individual biological differences, but immediate hair shedding within hours or a few days is not the expected pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.merckmanuals.com/en-ca/professional/multimedia/table/focal-radiation-injury" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: merckmanuals.com">[merckmanuals.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">merckmanuals.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Merck Manuals Table: Focal Radiation Injury*-Merck Manual Professional Edition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Merck Manuals Table: Focal Radiation Injury*-Merck Manual Professional Edition</span></span></span>

This matters because discussions of the Cash–Landrum case sometimes conflate two different questions:

* **Is delayed hair loss compatible with radiation?** Yes. A delay of roughly two to three weeks fits established radiation biology.
* **Does that delay prove radiation exposure?** No. Numerous medical conditions can also produce hair loss that develops over days or weeks after an illness or stressful event.

Consequently, the chronology alone neither confirms nor excludes radiation injury. It merely removes one possible objection—that radiation hair loss should have been immediate.

## Temporary versus permanent hair loss thresholds

Radiation epilation is not expected after trivial exposure. Published guidance from organisations such as the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), together with standard medical references, describes approximate threshold doses for local skin exposure.

Typical single-exposure thresholds include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Around 3–4 Gy (gray):** temporary epilation becomes possible, usually appearing after about 14–21 days.
* **Around 5–7 Gy or higher:** prolonged or permanent hair loss becomes increasingly likely, particularly if the dose is concentrated in one area.
* **Above roughly 10 Gy:** more severe skin reactions—including dry desquamation, blistering or ulceration at still higher doses—become progressively more likely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/PUB1891_web.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www-pub.iaea.org">[iaea.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www-pub.iaea.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Publications Safety Reports Series</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IAEA PublicationsSafety Reports Series...</span></span></span>

</div>

These figures are approximate rather than absolute. Human sensitivity varies, and clinical thresholds depend on factors including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* whether the exposure was delivered all at once or over time;
* the size of the irradiated scalp area;
* radiation type and energy;
* individual biological variation.

</div>

Nevertheless, the overall principle is robust: clinically significant radiation-induced scalp hair loss generally requires a relatively high **local absorbed dose**, not simply exposure to an unidentified source of radiation.

## Why an unmeasured dose limits the claim

This dose requirement is the strongest evidential limitation in arguments that Cash's [alopecia]({{ 'alopecia/' | relative_url }}) demonstrates radiation injury.

In documented radiation accidents and therapeutic radiology, clinicians often have at least one of the following:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* measured radiation exposure;(#endnote-8 "Endnote 8")<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.merckmanuals.com/en-ca/professional/injuries-poisoning/radiation-exposure-and-contamination/radiation-exposure-and-contamination" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: merckmanuals.com">[merckmanuals.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">merckmanuals.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Radiation Exposure and Contamination</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radiation Exposure and Contamination</span></span></span>
* treatment records showing delivered dose;
* physical dosimetry;
* reconstructed exposure estimates;
* characteristic skin changes that permit retrospective dose estimation.

</div>

No comparable dosimetric evidence exists for the Cash–Landrum incident.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Blqsx2c4ty4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: youtube.com">[youtube.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">youtube.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Full Guide to Acute Radiation Sickness (ARS) Symptoms &amp; Treatment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cash - Landrum UFO Encounter &#124; Dark Mysteries...</span></span></span>

Instead, the argument depends primarily on [reported symptoms]({{ 'reported-symptoms/' | relative_url }}) developing after the encounter. While those symptoms have been interpreted by some investigators as resembling radiation injury, no contemporaneous measurement established that Cash's scalp absorbed the several gray typically associated with deterministic epilation. Without that information, physicians cannot distinguish confidently between radiation-induced follicle damage and other medical explanations based solely on the timing of hair loss.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/PUB1983_web.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www-pub.iaea.org">[IAEA Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www-pub.iaea.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Publications IAEA HUMAN HEALTH SERIESNo. 42Establishing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IAEA PublicationsIAEA HUMAN HEALTH SERIESNo. 42Establishing...</span></span></span>

This absence of dose information is particularly important because deterministic radiation effects do not occur simply because radiation is present. They occur only after tissue-specific thresholds are exceeded.

## Timing is supportive but not diagnostic

The delayed appearance of substantial hair loss is therefore best viewed as a medically compatible finding rather than a diagnostic one.

If a patient were independently known to have received a local scalp dose of several gray, hair loss emerging after approximately two to three weeks would be expected and would strengthen the diagnosis of radiation epilation. In the Cash–Landrum case, however, the causal chain runs in the opposite direction: the hair loss itself is being used as evidence that such a dose occurred.

That reasoning is inherently weaker because delayed alopecia is not unique to radiation. Hair loss may follow severe illness, inflammatory disorders, autoimmune disease, medication effects or other physiological stresses, depending on the specific diagnosis. The observed timing therefore cannot by itself identify the underlying mechanism.

## What this means for the Cash–Landrum evidence

Within the broader debate over the witnesses' reported medical injuries, radiation epilation provides an instructive example of the difference between **compatibility** and **proof**.

The established medical literature supports three conclusions:

* Betty Cash's reported delayed hair loss is **not inconsistent** with radiation-induced epilation.
* Radiation epilation generally requires a **substantial local scalp dose**, typically measured in several gray, before clinically significant hair loss develops.
* Because no absorbed dose was measured or reliably reconstructed in the case, the timing of the alopecia cannot establish that radiation caused it. It remains one piece of circumstantial medical evidence rather than definitive proof of a radiation exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.merckmanuals.com/en-ca/professional/multimedia/table/focal-radiation-injury" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: merckmanuals.com">[merckmanuals.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">merckmanuals.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Merck Manuals Table: Focal Radiation Injury*-Merck Manual Professional Edition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Merck Manuals Table: Focal Radiation Injury*-Merck Manual Professional Edition</span></span></span>
