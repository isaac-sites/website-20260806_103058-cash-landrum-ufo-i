## Introduction

The illnesses reported after the Cash–Landrum incident have often been cited as evidence of ionising radiation exposure because they included nausea, weakness, eye irritation, blistering and later [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}). However, these symptoms are not unique to radiation. They also occur in heat injury, conventional burns, dehydration, severe stress, infection, medication reactions and numerous other medical conditions. Modern radiation medicine therefore does not diagnose radiation exposure from symptoms alone. Instead, clinicians combine the patient's symptoms with a credible exposure history, objective radiation measurements and laboratory evidence. Without those elements agreeing, symptoms by themselves cannot establish that ionising radiation was responsible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Signs and Symptoms of Radiation Exposure &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCJanuary 31, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 31, 2025</span></span></span>

## Symptoms Shared by Heat and Radiation

The principal difficulty in interpreting the Cash–Landrum witnesses' reported illnesses is that nearly every commonly discussed symptom belongs to a broad medical overlap rather than being a distinctive marker of radiation exposure.

Several reported features illustrate this problem:

* **Nausea and vomiting** occur after high-dose whole-body radiation exposure, but they are equally common in heat illness, dehydration, severe pain, anxiety, viral infections and many medications. Their presence alone provides no way to distinguish among these possibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/acute-radiation-syndrome.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">acute radiation syndrome</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 10, 2024</span></span></span>
* **Weakness and fatigue** are among the least specific symptoms in medicine. They accompany burns, fever, dehydration, inflammatory illness, emotional stress and countless other conditions, making them unsuitable as diagnostic evidence for radiation by themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/acute-radiation-syndrome.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">acute radiation syndrome</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 10, 2024</span></span></span>
* **Eye irritation** can result from intense heat, [bright light]({{ 'bright-light/' | relative_url }}), smoke, airborne irritants or inflammation. Although [eye symptoms]({{ 'eye-symptoms/' | relative_url }}) may occur after significant radiation exposure, they are not considered diagnostic without corroborating evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Signs and Symptoms of Radiation Exposure &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCJanuary 31, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 31, 2025</span></span></span>
* **Skin redness and blistering** develop after both thermal burns and cutaneous radiation injury. Their appearance alone does not reveal which mechanism produced the tissue damage. In fact, clinical guidance specifically evaluates whether a burn occurred without an identifiable heat source before suspecting radiation injury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/cutaneous-radiation-injury.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cutaneous radiation injury</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cutaneous Radiation Injuries &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 10, 2024</span></span></span>
* **Hair loss** may follow sufficiently high local radiation doses, but it is also associated with autoimmune disease, endocrine disorders, nutritional deficiencies, severe illness, psychological stress and many medications. Timing, distribution and associated laboratory findings are essential for interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDC...</span></span></span>

For the Cash–Landrum case, these overlapping symptoms mean that genuine illness does not automatically identify ionising radiation as the cause.

## Alternative Causes of Gastrointestinal and Eye Complaints

The witness accounts themselves describe intense heat, brilliant light and a vehicle becoming extremely hot. Those environmental factors introduce several plausible non-radiation explanations for many of the [reported symptoms]({{ 'reported-symptoms/' | relative_url }}).

Heat exposure can produce nausea, dizziness, weakness and vomiting through dehydration and heat stress, particularly when accompanied by emotional distress. Severe thermal exposure may also worsen existing medical conditions or trigger delayed inflammatory responses. None of these effects requires ionising radiation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Signs and Symptoms of Radiation Exposure &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCJanuary 31, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 31, 2025</span></span></span>

Similarly, painful eye irritation can arise from prolonged exposure to intense visible light, infrared radiation, hot air or smoke. Bright light and heat can inflame the conjunctiva and surrounding tissues without involving radioactive material. Because these mechanisms produce symptoms similar to those sometimes seen after radiation injury, clinicians rely on exposure history rather than symptoms in isolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Signs and Symptoms of Radiation Exposure &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCJanuary 31, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 31, 2025</span></span></span>

Even blistering skin does not settle the question. Conventional burns often deepen over hours after the initial injury, while cutaneous radiation injury may evolve over days or even weeks. The external appearance can overlap substantially, particularly during the early stages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/cutaneous-radiation-injury.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cutaneous radiation injury</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cutaneous Radiation Injuries &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 10, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why Exposure History and Medical Tests Must Agree

Radiation medicine is based on combining multiple independent lines of evidence rather than relying on symptoms.

Clinicians investigating suspected acute radiation syndrome consider questions such as:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[cdc.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDC...</span></span></span>

* Was there a known or measurable radiation source?
* Could the reported source physically deliver a sufficiently high dose?
* Did [symptom timing]({{ 'symptom-timing/' | relative_url }}) match recognised radiation syndromes?

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Were blood-cell counts consistent with radiation injury?<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/cutaneous-radiation-injury.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[cdc.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cutaneous radiation injury</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cutaneous Radiation Injuries &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 10, 2024</span></span></span>
* Did chromosome analysis or other biodosimetry indicate significant exposure?
* Were contamination surveys or dosimeter measurements available?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDC...</span></span></span>

</div>

The timing of symptoms is informative but not decisive. For example, nausea occurring shortly after exposure may raise suspicion if a documented high-dose radiation event has occurred. The same symptom in isolation has little diagnostic value because it is common across many illnesses. Likewise, skin changes are interpreted alongside dose estimates, laboratory findings and documented exposure circumstances rather than viewed as proof of radiation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/signs-symptoms/acute-radiation-syndrome.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">acute radiation syndrome</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 10, 2024</span></span></span>

This approach reflects an important principle in emergency radiation medicine: symptoms trigger investigation, but they do not by themselves confirm exposure.

## What This Means for the Cash–Landrum Incident

Within the debate over the Cash–Landrum incident, the reported illnesses remain compatible with the witnesses having experienced a genuine medical event. What they do not do, on their own, is identify ionising radiation as its cause.

Supporters of a radiation hypothesis sometimes point to the combination of nausea, skin injury and hair loss. However, radiation specialists would not regard that symptom cluster as diagnostic without supporting physical evidence, such as measured radiation, characteristic laboratory abnormalities or validated biodosimetry. Conversely, sceptics cannot dismiss the witnesses' illnesses simply because alternative explanations exist; overlapping symptoms mean that multiple causes remain medically plausible.

The key evidential gap is therefore not whether the reported symptoms were real, but whether objective evidence exists showing that a sufficiently intense source of ionising radiation was actually present. In the publicly available record, that corroborating evidence has not been demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDC...</span></span></span>
