## Introduction

One of the central logistical questions raised by the Cash–Landrum incident is not simply whether approximately twenty military-style helicopters were present, but where they could realistically have originated. Investigators quickly focused on military aviation facilities within reach of the reported location near Huffman, Texas. The leading candidates were Ellington Air Force Base (now Ellington Field Joint Reserve Base), the Army Reserve helicopter detachment at Tomball, and the much larger Army aviation presence at Fort Hood. Each possibility solves one part of the puzzle while creating another. Nearby bases reduce transit time but lacked large Chinook fleets, whereas Fort Hood possessed substantial Army aviation assets but lay far enough away to require careful planning, significant fuel, and a lengthy night flight.

Rather than identifying a confirmed launch point, the available evidence shows why investigators examined these locations and why none has been conclusively linked to the reported [helicopter formation]({{ 'helicopters-977f69/' | relative_url }}).

## Ellington and Tomball as Nearby Candidates

The strongest geographical candidates were the military aviation facilities closest to the reported encounter area.

Ellington, south-east of Houston, was already a major military installation supporting reserve and National Guard aviation during the period. Its proximity meant helicopters could reach the Huffman area relatively quickly, making it an obvious starting point for investigators. Historical records also show that Ellington continued to host military aviation units even after its transition away from an active Air Force base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/ellington-field" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tshaonline.org">[Texas State Historical Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tshaonline.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Texas State Historical Association Ellington Field</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Texas State Historical AssociationEllington FieldJune 1, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2017</span></span></span>

However, proximity alone did not solve the fleet question. According to the Army Inspector General memorandum reproduced by Patrick Gross's archive, investigators specifically contacted Major Dennis Haire regarding **eight Texas National Guard CH-47 Chinooks stationed at Ellington**. Flight records were reportedly checked, and investigators concluded there were no records showing aircraft operating in the relevant area that night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/cashlandrumsarran01.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: the Cash-Landrum case, 1980, USAF memo 1/4, Sarran...</span></span></span>

Tomball presented a different picture. The same memorandum identifies a nearby Army Reserve detachment under Chief Warrant Officer Gustafson operating **seven UH-1 Huey helicopters** from the civilian airfield at Tomball rather than Chinooks. Again, investigators reviewed available records and reported no matching flights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/cashlandrumsarran01.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: the Cash-Landrum case, 1980, USAF memo 1/4, Sarran...</span></span></span>

Taken together, these two nearby facilities illustrate an important logistical point:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Ellington had some Chinooks but apparently nowhere near enough to account for a formation approaching twenty aircraft by itself.
* Tomball was geographically convenient but operated a different helicopter type.
* Neither installation's available records reportedly supported a large-scale operation on 29 December 1980.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/cashlandrumsarran01.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: the Cash-Landrum case, 1980, USAF memo 1/4, Sarran...</span></span></span>

</div>

## Fort Hood: The Fleet Exists, but Distance Becomes the Problem

Fort Hood (now Fort Cavazos) naturally entered the discussion because it was one of the Army's largest aviation centres and possessed far greater helicopter resources than any Houston-area installation.

Unlike Ellington, Fort Hood had the organisational capacity to assemble a substantial number of Army helicopters if required. For that reason, Army investigators contacted Fort Hood's Inspector General and other aviation commands while attempting to identify any unit matching the witnesses' descriptions. The inquiry reportedly found no records connecting Fort Hood aircraft to the incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/cashlandrumsarran01.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: the Cash-Landrum case, 1980, USAF memo 1/4, Sarran...</span></span></span>

The larger difficulty is geography.

The reported encounter occurred roughly 160 miles (about 260 km) from Fort Hood. While a CH-47 Chinook has sufficient range to fly that distance, doing so is not equivalent to dispatching aircraft from a nearby base. A mission would require:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* pre-flight servicing;
* fuel planning for outbound and return legs;
* navigation for a sizeable night formation;
* communications across the route;
* coordination with air traffic authorities where appropriate; and
* sufficient aircraft availability at the originating unit.

</div>

The transit itself would also consume well over an hour under normal cruise speeds before any activity occurred near Huffman. That does not make such a mission impossible, but it transforms the reported formation into a deliberately organised operation rather than an impromptu local response.

## Could Multiple Bases Have Supplied the Formation?

One way to reconcile the apparent shortage of aircraft at nearby installations is to assume helicopters originated from more than one base.

In purely logistical terms, a combined force might appear more plausible than expecting a single installation to launch every aircraft. For example, Chinooks from Ellington could theoretically have been joined by aircraft from Fort Hood or another Army aviation unit while smaller helicopters departed from Tomball or elsewhere.

Such an arrangement, however, introduces new operational demands.

A multi-base launch would require participating units to agree on:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* departure times;
* rendezvous locations;
* altitude separation;
* radio frequencies;
* formation leadership;
* fuel reserves;
* emergency diversion procedures; and
* recovery plans after the mission.

</div>

Every additional departure point increases the complexity of the operation. Instead of solving the logistical problem, multiple launch bases shift it into one of large-scale coordination.

No documentary evidence has emerged showing such coordinated planning for the night of 29 December 1980. Army inquiries reportedly checked multiple commands precisely because investigators recognised that a dispersed origin was theoretically possible. Those enquiries nevertheless produced negative results.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/cashlandrumsarran01.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: the Cash-Landrum case, 1980, USAF memo 1/4, Sarran...</span></span></span>

## What the Official Investigation Actually Found

The Army's internal investigation did not limit itself to one installation.

According to the surviving memorandum, investigators contacted a wide range of organisations, including FORSCOM, Aviation Command, DARCOM, TECOM, Fort Hood, Corpus Christi maintenance facilities, Ellington National Guard personnel and Tomball Army Reserve personnel. Instead, it stated that investigators were unable to locate documentation supporting the specific large-scale activity alleged in the Cash–Landrum case after checking the commands most likely to have been involved.
