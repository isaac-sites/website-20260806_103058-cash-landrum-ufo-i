## Introduction

One of the recurring misunderstandings in discussions of the Cash–Landrum incident is the assumption that aircraft operating from an Air Force base must therefore have belonged to the US Air Force. Ellington Field, near Houston, demonstrates why that assumption is historically unreliable. Even during the late Cold War, Ellington hosted multiple military organisations, and Army aviation units could operate from the installation despite its long association with the Air Force. As a result, references to "Ellington Air Force Base" or "Ellington Field" do not, by themselves, identify which service owned or controlled a particular aircraft. This distinction became important when witnesses described large tandem-rotor helicopters resembling CH-47 Chinooks, an aircraft type associated with the Army rather than the Air Force.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/ellington-field" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tshaonline.org">[Texas State Historical Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tshaonline.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Texas State Historical Association Ellington Field</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Texas State Historical AssociationEllington FieldJune 1, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2017</span></span></span>

## The Texas Guard Chinooks based at Ellington

During the period surrounding the Cash–Landrum incident, Ellington was not simply an Air Force installation. It functioned as a shared military aviation centre supporting active, reserve and National Guard organisations. Texas Army National Guard aviation units were among the long-standing military tenants, and Army helicopters—including heavy-lift aircraft—could legitimately operate from the field without implying Air Force ownership.

The historical development of Ellington helps explain this arrangement. Originally an Army airfield during the First World War, it later became Ellington Air Force Base after the Air Force became an independent service in 1947. However, the installation continued to evolve into a multi-user facility rather than remaining an exclusively Air Force base. Texas Air National Guard units became major occupants, while Army National Guard, Army Reserve, NASA and other organisations also established permanent presences over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historycollection.jsc.nasa.gov/JSCHistoryPortal/history/ellington/Ellington.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historycollection.jsc.nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historycollection.jsc.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA/CR-1999-208921August 19, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2024</span></span></span>

The important implication for the Cash–Landrum discussion is straightforward. A Chinook seen departing from, returning to or associated with Ellington would not automatically have been an Air Force aircraft simply because the airfield had "Air Force Base" in its name. The aircraft could equally have belonged to an Army National Guard unit or another Army aviation organisation operating from the same installation.

## Ownership, basing and command are separate questions

The confusion often arises because three different concepts are treated as though they are interchangeable:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Ownership:** Which military service owns the aircraft type. The CH-47 Chinook has historically been an Army heavy-lift helicopter rather than an Air Force platform.
* **Basing:** Where the aircraft is stationed or temporarily operating. An aircraft can be based at, visit or stage through an installation operated by another service.
* **Command and control:** Which organisation directs a particular mission. National Guard aircraft may operate under state authority or federal authority depending on the mission and legal status.

</div>

These distinctions matter because a witness usually observes only the aircraft and its location, not its administrative ownership or command relationships. An observer who sees a large military helicopter near an installation widely known as an Air Force base may naturally assume that both the base and the aircraft belong to the Air Force, even though military administration works differently.

The National Guard further complicates the picture. Army National Guard aviation units are organised within the Army while simultaneously belonging to their respective states unless federalised. Consequently, identifying an Army helicopter does not automatically identify the specific command authority responsible for its operation on a particular day.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.147atkw.ang.af.mil/Portals/43/documents/Bio%20Environmental/Ellington%20Field%20JRB%20Community%20Involvement%20Plan%20Feb%2021.pdf?ver=tl4wLItZrzmzwSQozT0MnQ%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 147atkw.ang.af.mil">[147 ATKW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">147atkw.ang.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">147 ATKWAir National Guard</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">147 ATKWAir National Guard</span></span></span>

## Why an Air Force base name could mislead investigators

For investigators attempting to identify the reported helicopters, the name "Ellington Air Force Base" could easily become a misleading starting point.

An [Air Force denial]({{ 'air-force-denial/' | relative_url }}) would principally answer the question of whether Air Force aircraft or Air Force operations were involved. It would not necessarily resolve whether Army National Guard or Army Reserve aircraft based at the same installation had flown nearby. Recognising this distinction helps explain why later enquiries expanded beyond the Air Force and examined Army, Army National Guard and Army Reserve records rather than relying solely on Air Force files.

This does not establish that Army helicopters participated in the reported events. Rather, it explains why investigators could not logically stop after receiving an Air Force denial. If the reported aircraft resembled Chinooks, then the relevant organisations extended beyond the Air Force because the aircraft type itself pointed towards Army aviation. The later Army Inspector General enquiries reflected this broader institutional understanding.

## Ellington as a long-term joint military installation

Although Ellington's present-day organisation post-dates the 1980 incident, it illustrates a continuity that already existed in practice: the installation has long accommodated multiple military tenants with different parent services.

Modern descriptions of Ellington identify it as a joint military and civilian aviation facility supporting organisations from several branches, including the Texas Air National Guard, Texas Army National Guard, Army Reserve, Navy Reserve, Marine Corps Reserve and Coast Guard. Even though administrative arrangements have changed over the decades, the installation's history demonstrates that its identity has never been defined solely by the service reflected in an earlier base name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dvidshub.net/feature/jrbhouston" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dvidshub.net">[dvidshub.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dvidshub.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ellington Field Joint Reserve Base (Houston, TX)...</span></span></span>

This historical continuity reinforces the central analytical point: installation names describe places, not necessarily the ownership of every aircraft operating there.

## What this distinction means for the Cash–Landrum case

Within the broader question of [military ownership]({{ 'military-denials/' | relative_url }}) and agency boundaries, Ellington provides an important caution against overly simple conclusions.

The existence of Army aviation activity at an installation commonly identified with the Air Force means that neither witnesses nor investigators could reliably infer aircraft ownership from the base's historical title alone. Conversely, an Air Force statement denying involvement was meaningful evidence regarding Air Force operations but not definitive proof that no military helicopters of any kind were present.

For that reason, the Ellington example is best understood not as evidence that Army Chinooks were involved in the Cash–Landrum incident, but as an explanation of why "Air Force base" and "Air Force aircraft" are separate propositions. In a mixed-service environment, the location where an aircraft is seen and the organisation that owns or commands it are distinct questions that require separate evidence to answer.
