## Introduction

Within the Cash–Landrum incident, [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }})'s coat occupies a uniquely important place because it was reportedly used to open a car door that had become too hot to touch with a bare hand. If that account is accurate, the garment was the only publicly identified item said to have made deliberate, direct contact with a surface allegedly heated by the unidentified object. That made the coat potentially valuable for investigating two separate claims: whether the reported heat left measurable physical effects, and whether any radioactive or other hazardous material had been transferred to the fabric. Yet no publicly available record shows that the coat was promptly preserved, documented under a chain of custody, or subjected to laboratory analysis. As a result, one of the most promising pieces of physical evidence in the case effectively disappeared from the evidential record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

## Why the coat linked the heat and contamination claims

According to Betty Cash's contemporaneous account, she could not grasp [the car]({{ 'the-car/' | relative_url }}) door with her bare hand because it was painfully hot. Instead, she wrapped part of her leather coat around the handle to pull the door open before re-entering the vehicle. The detail appears repeatedly in early interviews and later retellings, making the coat more than an ordinary personal possession—it became an alleged contact surface between the witness and the claimed source of extreme heat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

Thermal damage alone would not demonstrate radiation exposure, while radioactive contamination would not by itself prove that the unidentified object caused it. Nevertheless, together they could have helped distinguish between ordinary heat, unusual environmental contamination and simple witness recollection.

## How clothing surveys and laboratory tests would have worked

Modern radiological response guidance treats clothing as one of the first items to evaluate because fabric can retain removable contamination after exposure to radioactive dust, droplets or debris. Emergency procedures call for contaminated clothing to be removed carefully, isolated in sealed containers and labelled so that later laboratory work can determine both the amount and location of any contamination. Removing clothing also prevents contamination from spreading and may eliminate a large proportion of external radioactive material from an affected person.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/prevention/self-decontaminate.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">self decontaminate</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How to Self-Decontaminate after a Radiation Emergency &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 15, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 15, 2024</span></span></span>

Had Cash's coat been preserved promptly, investigators could have performed several complementary examinations.

### Initial radiation survey

The first step would have been a systematic scan with calibrated radiation survey instruments to determine whether any areas of the garment produced readings above normal background. Surveyors would normally map elevated readings rather than simply record a single overall value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/screening/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Use of Radiation Detection, Measuring, and Imaging Instruments &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 27, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 27, 2024</span></span></span>

### Wipe testing

If elevated readings were found, technicians could perform wipe tests to determine whether radioactive material was removable from the leather surface or permanently fixed within it. This distinction matters because removable contamination suggests deposited material, whereas fixed contamination may indicate a different history or mechanism.

### Gamma spectroscopy

If measurable radioactivity were present, laboratory gamma spectroscopy could identify characteristic energy peaks associated with specific radionuclides rather than merely confirming that radiation existed. Identifying an isotope is often far more informative than recording an elevated count rate because it helps distinguish naturally occurring materials from industrial or medical sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/screening/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Use of Radiation Detection, Measuring, and Imaging Instruments &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 27, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 27, 2024</span></span></span>

### Materials examination

Even if no radioactivity were detected, forensic examination of the leather itself could have looked for:

* heat-induced cracking or shrinkage;
* chemical changes caused by intense heating;
* embedded particles;
* transferred residues from painted metal or other contacted surfaces.

</div>

These analyses would not have resolved the entire case but could have independently tested parts of the witness narrative.

## Why timing mattered

The evidential value of clothing declines rapidly once it returns to ordinary use.

Loose contamination can fall away during normal movement, be transferred to other objects or be removed during handling, storage or cleaning. Heat-related residues may also degrade over time through wear, sunlight and environmental exposure. For this reason, radiation emergency protocols emphasise preserving clothing immediately after an incident rather than days or weeks later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/prevention/self-decontaminate.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">self decontaminate</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How to Self-Decontaminate after a Radiation Emergency &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 15, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 15, 2024</span></span></span>

The distinction between exposure and contamination is especially important here. A person may receive a substantial external [radiation dose]({{ 'radiation-dose/' | relative_url }}) without carrying any radioactive material afterwards. Conversely, contaminated clothing can retain radioactive particles even if the wearer's radiation dose was comparatively low. ## What the missing chain of custody prevents investigators from proving

The greatest limitation is not simply the absence of laboratory results. It is the absence of documented evidence showing what happened to the coat after the incident.

A complete chain of custody would normally identify:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* when the garment was collected;
* who handled it;
* how it was packaged;
* whether contamination controls prevented cross-contamination;
* which laboratory examined it;
* what instruments were used;
* the calibration records;
* the analytical results.

</div>

No publicly available documentation establishes that this process occurred for Betty Cash's coat.

Without that documentation, later investigators cannot determine whether:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the coat was never tested;
* it was tested but produced negative findings that were never publicly released;
* it was examined only informally;
* it was discarded before meaningful analysis became possible.

</div>

These possibilities remain distinct, but the surviving public record does not allow them to be distinguished confidently.

## What the missing coat means for the wider evidence

Among the physical items associated with the Cash–Landrum incident, the coat stands out because it allegedly connected the witnesses directly to the claimed heat source. Unlike memories, sketches or [later testimony]({{ 'later-testimony/' | relative_url }}), it was a tangible object that could, in principle, have been independently examined.

Its absence does not prove that radioactive contamination existed, nor does it disprove the witnesses' account. Instead, it represents a lost opportunity. Had the garment been preserved under recognised forensic and radiological procedures, investigators might have obtained objective evidence bearing on the claims of intense heat, possible contamination, or both. Because that opportunity passed without a documented chain of custody or publicly available laboratory findings, the coat remains one of the clearest examples of how missing physical evidence continues to limit any scientific assessment of the Cash–Landrum incident.
