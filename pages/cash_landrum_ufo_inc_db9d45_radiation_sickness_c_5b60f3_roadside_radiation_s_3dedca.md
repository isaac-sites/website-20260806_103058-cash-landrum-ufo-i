## Introduction

One of the most frequently overlooked pieces of physical evidence in the [Cash–Landrum UFO incident]({{ 'cash-landrum-ufo-incident/' | relative_url }}) is not a positive finding but a negative one. Around nine months after the reported encounter, the Texas Department of Health's Bureau of Radiation Control examined the roadside where the witnesses said [the object]({{ 'the-object/' | relative_url }}) had hovered. Investigators found no measurable residual radioactive contamination in the area. That result is important because it places a significant constraint on claims that the location itself remained contaminated after the event. At the same time, it does not conclusively rule out every form of radiation exposure. The timing of the survey, the behaviour of different radioactive materials, and the distinction between irradiation and contamination all affect what a later field survey could reasonably have detected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wikidisc.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikidisc.org">[WikiDisc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikidisc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wiki Disc Cash–Landrum incident &#124; Wiki Disc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident &#124; WikiDiscJuly 1, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2026</span></span></span>

## How the site was reportedly examined

The state investigation followed requests for official assistance after the witnesses reported illnesses they believed were linked to the encounter. Texas Representative Larry Browder asked the Texas Department of Health to investigate, and the Bureau of Radiation Control assigned radiation specialist Charles Russ Meyer to conduct a field examination. On 16 September 1981, Meyer inspected the reported location along the road, carried out radiation measurements and collected soil samples for laboratory analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wikidisc.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikidisc.org">[WikiDisc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikidisc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wiki Disc Cash–Landrum incident &#124; Wiki Disc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident &#124; WikiDiscJuly 1, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2026</span></span></span>

According to both contemporary reporting and later summaries of the official investigation, the survey's principal objective was straightforward: determine whether any residual radioactive material could be detected at the site months after the reported event. Investigators reported that they found no abnormal residual radiation and that laboratory analysis of the soil likewise revealed no evidence of lingering radioactive contamination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blueblurrylines.com/2013/11/the-cash-landrum-incident-suppressed.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blueblurrylines.com">[Blue Blurry Lines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blueblurrylines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blueblurrylines.com.</span></span></span>

This finding differed from many later retellings of the case, which sometimes imply that physical traces of radiation were discovered at the location. The available documentation instead indicates that the state's radiation specialists reached the opposite conclusion: no measurable radioactive residue remained when they examined the scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blueblurrylines.com/2013/11/the-cash-landrum-incident-suppressed.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blueblurrylines.com">[Blue Blurry Lines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blueblurrylines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blueblurrylines.com.</span></span></span>

## What a negative survey can — and cannot — exclude

The roadside survey provides meaningful evidence, but its implications are narrower than they are sometimes presented.

A negative radiation survey strongly argues against a scenario in which long-lived radioactive contamination remained deposited on the roadway or surrounding soil. If substantial quantities of persistent radioactive material had been dispersed during the alleged incident, investigators would normally expect at least some detectable residual activity months later, depending on the isotope involved and environmental conditions. The absence of such findings therefore weakens claims that the site became permanently contaminated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wikidisc.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikidisc.org">[WikiDisc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikidisc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wiki Disc Cash–Landrum incident &#124; Wiki Disc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incident &#124; WikiDiscJuly 1, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1, 2026</span></span></span>

However, the survey does **not** automatically eliminate every conceivable radiation-related mechanism. A person can receive a [radiation dose]({{ 'radiation-dose/' | relative_url }}) without becoming contaminated. For example, exposure to penetrating X-rays or gamma rays ends when the source is removed, leaving no radioactive material behind. Likewise, contamination involving isotopes with very short half-lives may decay to undetectable levels long before investigators arrive.

For this reason, the survey answers one specific question—whether lasting radioactive contamination remained at the site—but cannot by itself determine whether a brief exposure occurred months earlier.

## Why timing and radiation type matter

The interval between the reported encounter in December 1980 and the state survey in September 1981 is central to interpreting the results.

Nearly nine months had elapsed before field measurements were taken. Investigators themselves reportedly recognised that if radioactive material with a short half-life had been involved, little or nothing might remain detectable after such a period. Meyer later explained that while no residual radioactive material was found, any contamination responsible for the witnesses' reported illnesses would have needed to involve material that decayed rapidly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blueblurrylines.com/2013/11/the-cash-landrum-incident-suppressed.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blueblurrylines.com">[Blue Blurry Lines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blueblurrylines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blueblurrylines.com.</span></span></span>

This distinction reflects basic principles of radiation physics:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Long-lived contamination** would be expected to leave measurable traces months later.
* **Short-lived radioactive isotopes** could decay below detectable levels before the survey.
* **External irradiation** from an intense but temporary source could leave no environmental residue at all once the source disappeared.

</div>

These possibilities mean that the survey's negative findings narrow the range of plausible explanations but do not identify which, if any, exposure mechanism actually occurred.

## How the survey fits into the wider medical debate

Within the broader debate over the witnesses' illnesses, the roadside survey functions mainly as a limiting piece of evidence rather than proof for either side.

Supporters of a radiation hypothesis sometimes note that the absence of residual contamination is compatible with a transient exposure or a rapidly decaying source. Critics, meanwhile, argue that the failure to detect environmental radioactivity adds to the lack of physical evidence linking the reported illnesses to ionising radiation. Both interpretations acknowledge the same factual result: investigators did not discover lasting radioactive contamination at the reported location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blueblurrylines.com/2013/11/the-cash-landrum-incident-suppressed.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blueblurrylines.com">[Blue Blurry Lines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blueblurrylines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blueblurrylines.com.</span></span></span>

The survey also illustrates the limits of retrospective environmental investigations. By the time measurements were taken, weather, traffic, natural dispersion and radioactive decay would all have reduced the chance of detecting any transient phenomenon, making the investigation inherently less definitive than measurements obtained immediately after the alleged event.

## What the roadside survey ultimately establishes

The Texas Department of Health's investigation produced one of the few official environmental examinations associated with the Cash–Landrum case. Its principal finding was clear: no abnormal residual radiation was detected in field measurements or analysed soil samples months after the reported encounter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.blueblurrylines.com/2013/11/the-cash-landrum-incident-suppressed.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blueblurrylines.com">[Blue Blurry Lines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blueblurrylines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on blueblurrylines.com.</span></span></span>

That result substantially weakens claims that the site retained persistent radioactive contamination. At the same time, because the survey occurred many months later and because not all forms of radiation exposure leave lasting environmental signatures, it cannot definitively exclude every short-duration exposure scenario. As a result, the roadside survey is best understood as an important negative dataset: it constrains the range of plausible explanations without, on its own, resolving the question of what caused the witnesses' reported medical symptoms.
