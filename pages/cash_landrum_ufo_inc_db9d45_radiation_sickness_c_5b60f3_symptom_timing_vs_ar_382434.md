## Introduction

One of the strongest ways to assess the Cash–Landrum radiation claim is not simply to ask whether the [reported symptoms]({{ 'reported-symptoms/' | relative_url }}) resemble acute radiation syndrome (ARS), but whether they appeared in the order and at the times clinicians would expect after a substantial radiation exposure. Timing is a central diagnostic clue in radiation medicine because different tissues respond at different rates, and the interval between exposure and symptoms often helps estimate both the severity and type of injury.

The reported chronology from the Cash–Landrum incident only partially matches recognised radiation patterns. Some early symptoms are compatible with acute radiation exposure, but later reports of skin injury and [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}) are less straightforward when compared with established clinical timelines. This does not prove or disprove the witnesses' account, but it means the sequence itself provides mixed rather than unequivocal support for a diagnosis of acute radiation syndrome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 23, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2024</span></span></span>

## When the witnesses said symptoms began

Accounts from investigators, witness statements and later summaries broadly describe a sequence in which [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }}) experienced severe headache, eye irritation, nausea and vomiting soon after the reported encounter, followed by progressive weakness and painful skin changes over the following days. Hair loss became one of the most widely repeated features of the case, but surviving summaries of the [hospital record]({{ 'hospital-record/' | relative_url }}) suggest that significant hair loss was not a prominent finding on admission and became more noticeable later.

That distinction matters because many popular retellings compress the illness into a single dramatic episode in which vomiting, burns and hair loss all appear almost immediately. The available medical summaries instead describe a more staggered progression, making it necessary to compare each symptom separately rather than treating the illness as one continuous radiation syndrome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 23, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2024</span></span></span>

Equally important is the uncertainty surrounding the precise timeline. Much of the chronology comes from retrospective accounts written after the event rather than detailed contemporaneous hospital documentation. As a result, investigators can compare the reported sequence with known radiation injuries, but they cannot reconstruct an exact clinical timeline with confidence.

## Typical phases of acute radiation syndrome

Acute radiation syndrome has a characteristic, although dose-dependent, progression after a sufficiently large whole-body exposure.

The first phase is the **prodromal stage**, during which nausea, vomiting and sometimes diarrhoea appear. At higher doses these symptoms may begin within minutes or hours; at lower but still clinically significant doses they may be delayed for several hours. Earlier vomiting generally suggests a higher absorbed dose, although it is only an approximate indicator because anxiety, trauma and other illnesses can also trigger similar symptoms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 23, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2024</span></span></span>

This is usually followed by a **latent phase**, during which the patient often appears to improve despite underlying biological injury continuing to develop. Depending on dose, this apparently well period can last from several hours to weeks before the major illness phase emerges. The existence of this temporary recovery is one of the classic features of ARS.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 23, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2024</span></span></span>

When skin receives a high local [radiation dose]({{ 'radiation-dose/' | relative_url }}), clinicians also recognise a separate pattern known as **cutaneous radiation injury**. Early redness or warmth may appear within hours, sometimes fading before a second wave of inflammation develops days or weeks later. Blistering, ulceration and tissue breakdown generally require substantially higher local skin doses and usually evolve over days to weeks rather than immediately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/cri.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cutaneous Radiation Injury (CRI): Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDC...</span></span></span>

Hair loss follows its own biological timetable. Radiation damages the rapidly dividing cells in hair follicles, but existing hairs are not shed instantly. Clinical guidance places radiation-induced epilation at approximately **14 to 21 days** after exposure, with temporary hair loss generally requiring a skin dose of about 3 grays or more.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/cri.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cutaneous Radiation Injury (CRI): Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDC...</span></span></span>

## Where the reported chronology fits

The earliest reported symptoms are among the more compatible aspects of the Cash–Landrum case.

If Betty Cash experienced nausea and vomiting shortly after the alleged encounter, that timing falls within the recognised prodromal window for sufficiently large radiation exposures. Eye irritation, headache and general malaise are also non-specific symptoms that can accompany many illnesses, including radiation exposure, although they are not diagnostic by themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome: Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDCApril 23, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 23, 2024</span></span></span>

Similarly, early skin redness or swelling would not necessarily conflict with radiation injury. ## Where the chronology becomes difficult

The more problematic issue is the relationship between the reported [early illness]({{ 'early-illness/' | relative_url }}) and the later appearance of hair loss and skin lesions.

Radiation-induced hair loss is not expected to occur immediately after exposure. Instead, damaged follicles usually require one to three weeks before affected hairs are shed. If later reports accurately indicate that Betty Cash's most substantial hair loss developed after hospital admission rather than on the first day, that timing is actually more consistent with recognised radiation biology than the simplified versions often repeated in UFO literature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/cri.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cutaneous Radiation Injury (CRI): Information for Clinicians &#124; Radiation Emergencies &#124; CDC...</span></span></span>

## Why symptom timing cannot settle the case on its own

Chronology is an important diagnostic tool, but it is not definitive in isolation.

Several reported features of the witnesses' illness fall within recognised windows for radiation injury, particularly the rapid onset of gastrointestinal symptoms and the delayed development of hair loss. Other aspects are less convincing, especially the uncertain evolution of the skin lesions and the lack of objective evidence expected after a sufficiently large whole-body exposure.

For clinicians, symptom timing is only one part of a broader assessment that includes measured radiation dose, blood-cell changes, physical examination and exposure history. In the Cash–Landrum incident, those additional pieces of evidence remain incomplete or unavailable. Consequently, the reported sequence neither confirms nor excludes acute radiation syndrome.
