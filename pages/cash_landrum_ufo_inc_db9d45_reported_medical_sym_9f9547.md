## Introduction

All three witnesses in the Cash–Landrum incident said they became ill after returning from the reported encounter, but their accounts describe sharply different levels of severity. Betty Cash reported the fastest, most extensive and most prolonged illness: burning skin, intense thirst, swelling, gastrointestinal distress, weakness, headaches, blistering and later hair loss, followed by hospital treatment. The surviving record therefore supports a cautious conclusion: the witnesses consistently alleged a cluster of real physical complaints after the event, with Cash by far the most seriously affected. It does not, however, establish that radiation caused those symptoms. Much of the detailed evidence comes from witness testimony recorded months later, investigator summaries and incomplete public accounts of private [medical records]({{ 'medical-record/' | relative_url }}).

## What happened after they returned home?

The witnesses said their discomfort began either during the journey home or shortly afterwards. In an August 1981 interview at Bergstrom Air Force Base, Cash stated that she felt ill and intensely hot within about 30 minutes of the encounter. She compared the sensation to having been badly sunburnt and said that by the following morning she was too weak to get out of bed, extremely thirsty and seemingly dehydrated. Her lips, ears and body reportedly became markedly swollen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

The immediate complaints reported across the three accounts were:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* burning, watering or reddened eyes;
* skin redness and a sunburn-like sensation;
* nausea and upset stomach;
* diarrhoea and abdominal pain;
* severe weakness, fatigue and loss of energy;
* thirst, dry mouth and apparent dehydration;
* headaches and general bodily discomfort.

</div>

These symptoms were not described with equal precision by each witness. Cash and Vickie gave the most developed retrospective accounts. Colby told the Air Force interviewers that he felt nothing unusual during the sighting itself, but awoke the next morning with what he called a “bad virus”, including diarrhoea and severe stomach pain. He could not remember whether he had vomited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

The timing matters because some later retellings compress the illness into a single, uniform episode. The interview record instead suggests a sequence: acute gastrointestinal and heat-like complaints first, followed over subsequent days or weeks by skin lesions, continuing eye problems and [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}). That sequence was clearest in Cash’s account but was also alleged, in milder form, by Vickie and Colby.

## Why was Betty Cash’s illness considered the most severe?

Cash said she had remained outside [the car]({{ 'the-car/' | relative_url }}) longer than the others during the encounter. Whether or not that explains the medical difference, her subsequent account was plainly the gravest. She reported extensive blistering, with fluid-filled lesions that broke open, persistent diarrhoea and stomach upset, severe weakness, headaches, sensitivity to sunlight and heat, and difficulty functioning independently. By August 1981, she said that hot baths or prolonged sunlight could make her skin appear burnt again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

She also reported substantial swelling. According to [her testimony]({{ 'her-testimony/' | relative_url }}), her lips and ears enlarged dramatically and relatives initially had difficulty recognising her in hospital. She stated that doctors at Parkway Hospital in Houston conducted numerous tests but did not identify an underlying condition that explained the full episode. These comments establish what Cash told Air Force personnel; they should not be mistaken for an independent medical finding that all alternative causes had been excluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

Cash described spending roughly a month in hospital, although the length of the initial admission varies in later accounts and summaries. During the same interview, she also referred to enduring 13 days in hospital before telling her doctor about the encounter. Reviews drawing on the medical chronology have reported an admission in early January 1981 lasting into the middle of that month, followed by further treatment. The discrepancy illustrates why exact hospital durations should be presented cautiously rather than repeated as a settled figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

Hair loss became one of the best-known features of Cash’s case, reinforced by photographs and her later use of a wig. Yet surviving descriptions of the early clinical notes complicate the popular version. A later critical review reports that her attending physician observed little or no hair loss when she was first admitted and that significant loss developed afterwards. The same review says the initial dermatological assessment recorded swelling and [cellulitis]({{ 'cellulitis/' | relative_url }}) of the scalp and face rather than the extensive loss of skin sometimes described in dramatic retellings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash%E2%80%93Landrum_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cash–Landrum incidentThe Cash–Landrum Incident was an unidentified flying object sighting in the United States in 1980, which witnesses c...</span></span></span>

That distinction does not show that Cash was well. It shows that the chronology and clinical labels matter. “She later lost hair” is better supported than claims that she arrived at hospital already losing large clumps. Likewise, blistering and swelling appear repeatedly in testimony, but the most severe descriptions are not all reproduced in publicly accessible contemporaneous hospital documentation.

## How did Vickie and Colby’s symptoms differ?

Vickie Landrum described a significant but less disabling illness. She reported diarrhoea, severe nausea, arm blisters and lesions, eye swelling and watering, a persistent gritty sensation in her eyes, and skin reactions that reappeared after sunlight exposure. During the Air Force interview, an officer noted visible scars, blisters or discoloured areas on her lower arms and hand. This is one of the relatively rare moments when the transcript records an interviewer’s direct observation rather than only repeating a retrospective complaint.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

Vickie also claimed that her fingernails came off and that her hair began falling out approximately four to six weeks after the encounter. She said the hair later grew back with a different texture. Her eye complaints were especially prominent: she described months of watering and swelling and said an eye doctor had warned that a cataract-like condition might eventually require surgery. The transcript records those statements, but it does not contain the examining doctor’s original notes or establish that the eye changes were caused by the reported object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

Colby’s reported illness was milder but not trivial. He described stomach pain and diarrhoea the following morning, continuing intermittent stomach trouble, reddening of the eyes in a swimming pool, rapid sunburn and a small bald patch at the crown of his head. When prompted, he also acknowledged having had a facial blister that reportedly reopened or “popped” after sun exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

Vickie’s description of Colby was more dramatic than his own. She said he temporarily lost bowel control, behaved like a much younger child for more than a month and developed hanging water blisters on one side of his face. The difference does not necessarily make either account false: Colby was seven, the interview occurred many months later, and an adult carer may remember symptoms a child cannot. It does mean that claims about his condition should identify whether they came from Colby directly or from Vickie speaking for him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

## Which symptoms are strongest in the surviving record?

The surviving evidence is not a single complete medical file. It consists of witness interviews, reported hospital treatment, investigator reconstructions, legal material and later commentary. Within that uneven record, some claims are better established than others.

**Most consistently reported:** gastrointestinal illness, weakness, eye irritation, skin redness or burning, and later skin lesions appear repeatedly in the witnesses’ own accounts. Cash’s hospitalisation is also firmly part of the documented aftermath, even though precise dates and durations are sometimes reported inconsistently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

**Reported with plausible timing but incomplete clinical verification:** delayed hair loss was alleged by all three, although Colby described only a very small patch. Vickie placed the beginning of her hair loss about a month or more after the event. Delayed epilation can occur after substantial radiation exposure to the skin, with the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention giving a typical onset of roughly 14 to 21 days at sufficiently high local doses. That compatibility in timing is not proof that radiation was involved, because hair loss has numerous other causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

**More weakly established or commonly overstated:** claims that physicians conclusively diagnosed [radiation sickness]({{ 'radiation-claim/' | relative_url }}), that all three had the same syndrome, or that later cancer and cataracts were demonstrated consequences of the encounter go beyond what the accessible records show. Cash and Vickie clearly believed radiation was responsible, and the symptom list overlaps with recognised features of acute radiation syndrome and cutaneous radiation injury. However, the CDC stresses that acute radiation syndrome has no unique sign or symptom and requires a high, short-duration dose of penetrating radiation affecting most of the body.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

A rigorous radiation diagnosis would ordinarily depend on more than nausea, diarrhoea, redness and hair loss. Investigators would want a reliable exposure source, dose information, serial blood counts, characteristic changes in white blood cells, a clinically coherent progression and exclusion of competing diagnoses. The publicly available Cash–Landrum material does not provide that complete chain.

There is also a dose problem. Very rapid nausea and severe whole-body illness can indicate a substantial radiation dose, while profound skin injury may imply an intense local exposure. But an exposure severe enough to produce the most extreme versions of all the reported symptoms might also be expected to cause clearer haematological and systemic consequences. Conversely, heat, ultraviolet or infrared energy could potentially cause eye and skin injury without producing classic whole-body radiation syndrome, but would not readily explain every gastrointestinal complaint. The evidence therefore leaves mechanism and causation unresolved.

## What the medical claims do—and do not—prove

The symptom record is important because it separates the Cash–Landrum case from an encounter story with no aftermath. Three named witnesses described illness, one underwent substantial hospital treatment, and an official interview preserved detailed claims about gastrointestinal distress, eye injury, blistering, fatigue and hair loss. The differences in severity are also broadly consistent across the accounts: Cash suffered most, Vickie less, and Colby had the mildest and least clearly documented course.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transcript, Cash-Landrum Interview...</span></span></span>

What the record does not prove is the nature of the exposure. Symptom resemblance is not a diagnosis, and sequence alone does not establish cause. The accessible documents contain no confirmed radiation measurement from the witnesses, no securely reconstructed dose and no publicly available complete set of contemporaneous clinical records for all three. Later summaries that describe “documented radiation injuries” therefore state the conclusion more strongly than the evidence permits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/hcp/clinical-guidance/ars.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS) (sometimes known as acute radiation sickness) is an acute illness caused by radiation exposure (or irradia...</span></span></span>

The most defensible reading is narrower. Cash, Vickie and Colby reported becoming ill soon after the alleged encounter. Their complaints included acute gastrointestinal upset, irritated eyes, skin changes and weakness, followed in some accounts by blisters, heat or sunlight sensitivity and delayed hair loss. Cash’s illness was severe enough to require hospital care and remained central to her life afterwards. The suffering may have been genuine and medically significant while the cause remained unknown—a distinction essential to understanding both the strength and the limitation of the case.
