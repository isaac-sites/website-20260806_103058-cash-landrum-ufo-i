## Introduction

[Colby Landrum]({{ 'colby-landrum/' | relative_url }}) was only seven years old when the Cash–Landrum incident allegedly occurred in December 1980. By the time he was formally interviewed by Air Force personnel at Bergstrom Air Force Base on 17 August 1981, more than seven months had passed, and he was questioned in the same setting as the two adult witnesses, Betty Cash and [Vickie Landrum]({{ 'vickie-landrum/' | relative_url }}). That timing and interview structure are important because they make it difficult to distinguish which parts of Colby's account reflected his own spontaneous recollection and which may have been shaped by adult discussion or by the interviewers' questions. The issue is not whether Colby was honest, but whether the interview format preserved an independent childhood memory in a way that modern research would regard as reliable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

## How Much of Colby's Account Was Spontaneous?

The surviving transcript shows that Colby did provide answers in his own words, but these were frequently embedded within a conversation dominated by adults. Air Force officers addressed questions to him, yet his grandmother and [Betty Cash]({{ 'betty-cash/' | relative_url }}) often answered first, expanded on his replies, or supplied details that he did not initially mention. Rather than isolating the child's recollection, the interview captured a family discussion about a shared experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because memory researchers generally separate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Free recall**, where a witness narrates events with minimal interruption.
* **Prompted recall**, where answers emerge in response to increasingly specific questions.
* **Suggestive questioning**, where the wording itself implies an expected answer.

</div>

The Bergstrom interview relied heavily on prompted recall rather than uninterrupted narrative, making it more difficult to identify which details originated from Colby himself.

## The Blister Question and Colby's Initial Uncertainty

One of the clearest examples concerns questions about Colby's physical condition after the reported encounter.

When interviewers explored his injuries, Colby's responses were comparatively brief and sometimes uncertain. Adults around him supplied additional information about blisters, [hair loss]({{ 'hair-loss/' | relative_url }}), illness and later medical concerns, often before he had fully answered or instead of allowing him to describe events in his own sequence. The transcript repeatedly shifts between direct questions to Colby and explanatory answers from Vickie Landrum or Betty Cash, reducing the opportunity to distinguish his independent recollections from family testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

The issue is not that the adults were necessarily inaccurate. Rather, once an adult provides details in front of a child, it becomes increasingly difficult for later investigators to determine whether subsequent agreement reflects the child's original memory or acceptance of information supplied by trusted adults.

## Why Children Are Sensitive to Trusted Adult Prompts

Modern developmental psychology provides a useful framework for interpreting this interview.

Research consistently finds that children are capable eyewitnesses, particularly when encouraged to give uninterrupted accounts. However, they are also more likely than adults to incorporate information suggested by authority figures when memories have weakened over time or when interviews rely on specific, leading questions instead of open invitations to remember. Interviewer expectations can unintentionally influence children's responses, especially after long delays between an event and formal questioning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17605517/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Developmental differences in the effects of repeated interviews and interviewer bias on young children&#x27;s event memory and false rep...</span></span></span>

Several features of the Air Force interview match circumstances that contemporary researchers regard as increasing the difficulty of evaluating an independent childhood memory:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the interview occurred roughly seven months after the event;
* Colby had discussed the experience with the adults who accompanied him;
* questioning took place in the adults' presence rather than separately;
* adults frequently elaborated on or confirmed his answers during the interview itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

</div>

None of these factors demonstrates that Colby's recollections became inaccurate. They do, however, reduce confidence that investigators successfully preserved an uncontaminated record of what a seven-year-old independently remembered.

## What a Modern Child Interview Would Do Differently

Current forensic interviewing practice has developed considerably since 1981. Although protocols vary internationally, widely used evidence-based approaches share several principles intended to maximise reliable recall while minimising suggestion.

A modern interviewer would typically:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* interview the child separately from adult witnesses;
* begin with broad prompts such as "Tell me everything you remember";
* avoid introducing details the child has not already mentioned;
* delay specific questions until after free narrative is exhausted;
* discourage adults from answering on the child's behalf;
* document exactly which information originated from the child rather than from accompanying adults.

</div>

These practices are designed to preserve the distinction between spontaneous memory and prompted recollection, allowing later investigators to assess how each factual detail entered the record.

## What the Interview Can—and Cannot—Tell Us

The Bergstrom transcript remains an important historical document because it records Colby's participation relatively early in the public history of the Cash–Landrum case. It shows that he personally described aspects of the experience and that Air Force personnel questioned him directly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/cashlani.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Cufon]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bergstrom AFB Interview of Betty Cash, Vickie &amp; Colby Landrum, Part 1 of 2August 17, 1981...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 17, 1981</span></span></span>

At the same time, the interview cannot establish with confidence which parts of his testimony were entirely spontaneous. The combination of delayed questioning, continuous adult involvement and repeated prompted recall means that his account is best interpreted alongside modern research on children's memory rather than as a pristine record of an untouched childhood recollection. This does not imply fabrication or deliberate coaching.
